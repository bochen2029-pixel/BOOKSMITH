## X. Norther

Say it's Friday, the thirtieth, the last weekday of the odd week, and the first norther of the year comes through at twenty to five.

From the forty-first floor you see it before you feel it. The sky to the northwest goes the color of a nickel. The flags on the plaza across Commerce, which have hung straight down all week, lift and straighten and point south, all at once, like something that has made up its mind. On the screen the temperature outside goes 79, 71, 64, 57 in forty minutes, and the windows tick as they cool. Out past Irving, without looking, you know what the airport is doing. It's turning around. Every aircraft over the Metroplex is being told, one at a time, in a calm voice, that it will now land from the south. The line of lights you've watched all week will re-form on the other side, over Euless, over the house you grew up in, where your father will hear the first heavy come over at a quarter past five and know what it means before the weather does.

The week went the way weeks go when they go well, which is to say quickly and without much to show for it. Wednesday at seven the two of you sat at your desk with bad coffee and found the sixes. There were fourteen. The last one was in a configuration file nobody had opened since May, and when she found it she said nothing at all, just turned the screen toward you and put her finger on it. At nine she stood up in front of the council with a slide titled *What we learned* where the hole had been, and they asked two questions, and she answered three. Thursday there was dinner with the whole group at a place in Deep Ellum where you could hear nothing and said nothing and watched her be good at it from the far end of the table. Today there were meetings. Her bag is already at the front desk of the hotel on Elm. Her flight is the last London flight of the night, out of Terminal D at twenty to ten, and there's a car booked for a quarter past seven, a real one this time.

She comes by your desk at half past six with the coat on.

"It earned its keep," she says.

"It did."

"Fifty-five degrees. I was promised Texas."

"That is Texas. That's the other one."

She looks out the window at the city, which has gone hard-edged and clear in the cold, every light sharper than it was. The flags are still straight out.

"I'm told there's a car at a quarter past."

"There is."

You've been deciding this since Tuesday, and you find that you decided it some time ago without telling yourself.

"Before you go," you say. "Can I show you something? It's downstairs. Twenty minutes."

"Is it a secret?"

"It's a sandbox."

"That's not a no."

"No," you say. "It isn't."

The elevator takes you down forty-one floors, and your ears do what they do, and then it keeps going. Past L. P1, P2, P3. P4, where your car is. The doors open on the bottom of the garage, lit the way the bottoms of garages are lit, and the air doesn't move.

"How far down does this go?" she asks.

"This is as far as it goes. The company rents the smallest room on the lowest floor. It was cheap because nobody wanted it."
