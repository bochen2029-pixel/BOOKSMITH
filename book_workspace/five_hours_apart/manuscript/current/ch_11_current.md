## XI. The Smallest Room

You walk her across the bottom of the garage, past your own car, which unlocks as you pass because it thinks you've come for it, and locks again when you haven't. At the far wall there's a door marked for staff, and behind it a corridor that nobody has painted since the building was new, lit by the kind of tubes that hum. It runs farther than it should, past doors with the names of tenants who left years ago. Halfway along, it bends, and at the bend there's a sealed steel door with a sign on it that says the tunnels are closed. Downtown used to be connected underneath, a city under the city where people walked from building to building in the summer without ever seeing the sun, and then the city decided it wanted them on the street and bricked most of it up. The air down here still moves as though it remembers somewhere to go.

Your door has no number. It has a strip from a label maker, stuck slightly crooked, that says KELVIN.

"Who's Kelvin?"

"A man who thought atoms were knots in a fluid. In 1867."

"Was he right?"

"No. Not about atoms."

You badge in. The lock thinks about it and lets you.

The room is colder than the corridor and much quieter than she expected; you can see her expecting a roar and not getting one. It's the size of a hotel bathroom. There's a short rack with two machines in it, each the height of a bread box, each holding four cards, with a loom of cables between them that you dressed yourself one Saturday with zip ties and a podcast. There's a folding table, a chair, a monitor, a keyboard, a space heater you have never once turned on, and, taped to the wall above the monitor, a sheet of paper with a column of numbers in your handwriting that you have not updated since August. The air handler in the ceiling breathes slowly. The machines, at the moment, don't.

"Is it off?"

"No. It's quiet. Nothing's due."

On the monitor is a sheet of color the size of a door, and it isn't moving.

It takes her a moment to see it, the way it takes a moment to see a stereogram. It's a field of tiny squares, millions of them, each one a single flat color, and the colors run through each other in slow bands and whorls, red into orange into green into blue and back to red, like oil on water that has stopped. Here and there the bands tighten into a point where every color meets at once, a pinwheel the size of a fingernail, and nearly every pinwheel has a partner a little way off turning the other way.

"What am I looking at?"

"Every square is one cell. Every cell holds an angle, like a compass needle. The color is which way it's pointing. Neighbors want to point the same way, so you get the bands. The pinwheels are where it can't be made to agree. Those are the only things in it that can't drift. They can be born, or they can die, and only in pairs. Those are its concepts."

She looks for a long time.

"That's a lot of compass needles."

"A billion and change. It's cut into eight pieces, one per card. It doesn't know it's in eight pieces."

"And what is it doing?"

"Right now? Nothing. It's held at a temperature where things can form and hold. Just inside the edge. Any colder and nothing new can happen. Any warmer and nothing holds. There's a loop that keeps every region of it just inside the edge, and the loop is the only part of this I wrote that I'd call clever. The rest is physics from the seventies."

"Whose physics?"

"Kosterlitz. Thouless. Berezinskii. Thin films of helium. Arrays of superconductors." You stop, because you can hear yourself starting the other speech, the one you've given no one. "I can tell you the things or I can show you one."

"Show me one."

You point at the top of the monitor, at a line of small type she hasn't noticed. *Quiet. Frontier 0. Idle 790 W.*

"That's what it costs to exist. Eight cards doing nothing. About a space heater on low." You nod at the space heater. "Less than that one."

"Why is that worth pointing at?"

"Because a language model costs the same whether it's thinking or not, and this costs nothing until something happens, and then it costs exactly what the something reaches. Wait."

You wait. She waits. She's good at it; you noticed that on Tuesday at the rail, before you knew her, in the way she read the hall.

Then the machines take a breath.

It's the fans. They come up from nothing to something, a low exhale, for perhaps half a second, and on the monitor a patch of color on the eastern edge of the sheet shivers, a ripple that runs across a few hundred cells and dies, and the line of type says *Frontier 14* and then *Frontier 3* and then *Quiet*, and the fans go back down, and the room is as it was.

"What was that?"

"A plane."

She turns and looks at you.

"There's a receiver on my windowsill upstairs. Thirty dollars. The size of a thumb. Every aircraft within about a hundred and fifty miles broadcasts where it is, how high, which way, once a second. That's what it hears. That's its only sense, apart from a clock and its own temperature. One of them just crossed the edge of what the antenna can see, and it reached in exactly that far, and stopped."

"It knows they're planes."

"It doesn't know what a plane is. It doesn't have the knot for it. It knows that something comes down out of its northeast every evening around seven that isn't like the others, and that something heavy climbs out that way at nine, and that at one in the morning its south goes busy with things that come in low and don't leave." You stop. "Cargo. It doesn't know that either."

"How long has it been listening?"

"Since June. The fourteenth."

"On the company's cards."

"On the sandbox. It's on the schedule under my name. Test and dev."

"Does Dana know?"

"Dana knows I use the sandbox."

"That's not the same."

"No."

She doesn't say anything to that, and you're grateful. She goes back to the screen.

"Why *is* that worth pointing at? The breath."

"Because a lava lamp costs the same all night. This costs nothing when the sky is empty and something when the sky changes, and the something tracks the sky. That's the first thing I had to know. Whether it was a very expensive lava lamp."

"And it isn't."

"It isn't that."
