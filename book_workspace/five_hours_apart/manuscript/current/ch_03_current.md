## III. International Parkway

Say it's eight minutes to eight when you come through the south toll plaza and down International Parkway.

The airport is bigger than Manhattan. You've known that your whole life as trivia, and you feel it now, with the road running straight up the middle of the place and the terminals curving away on either side, each one a long lit crescent, like ships moored along a dark canal. They built them as half circles so the walk from the car to the gate would be short, by people who assumed everyone would always drive. You know this place the way you know a house you grew up in. You know that the train runs inside security and the bus runs outside it, that the garages sit inside the crescents, that D is on the left going north, that the cell lot fills up by eight on a Friday. Out beyond the terminals, aircraft move across the field slowly and deliberately, as though the ground were deep water.

You leave the car on the fourth level of the Terminal D garage, between a minivan and a black sedan whose driver is asleep with his seat tipped all the way back, and you walk in.

Inside, it's the hour when an airport stops performing. Shops are pulling down their grilles. A man rides a floor polisher in slow loops across the terrazzo. The announcements come less often and sound like they're being made to an empty room. Up on the board, beside LONDON HEATHROW, the word DELAYED flickers and becomes LANDED 8:14, and then, a while later, AT GATE.

She'll have come in on a triple-seven, the big one, the one with the raked wingtips, and you know the choreography from here. The walk off the jet bridge. The lines at passport control, which at this hour are short but never nothing. The wait at the carousel. Then the frosted doors. Forty minutes if everything goes right, and you have never once seen everything go right.

So you have forty minutes, and a long day behind you, and you find the vending machines down by baggage claim, lit up blue in an alcove like a small aquarium. You press the buttons for the orange can, the Ultra Sunrise, and it drops with that heavy hollow sound, and it's cold enough to hurt your hand a little. You crack it standing right there. The hiss. The first long swallow, citrus and cold and faintly, pleasantly chemical, like something built in a lab by people who liked you. Three minutes later it arrives behind your eyes, a lift, a brightening, as if someone turned the gain up on the whole terminal. You weren't tired, and now you're less tired than that. It's a small pleasure and a cheap one and completely reliable.

International arrivals come out downstairs, through a pair of frosted doors that slide open every minute or two, release a few people blinking into the light, and close again. In front of the doors, behind a rail, a crowd has gathered. There are drivers in dark suits holding tablets with names on them. There's a family with a handmade sign in purple glitter, an old man in a pressed shirt with grocery-store flowers still in their plastic sleeve, and two small boys taking turns climbing the rail until their mother stops them and they start again.

Each time the doors open, the whole crowd leans in. Each time, someone's face changes.

A young woman comes through, and the old man with the flowers makes a sound you weren't meant to hear, and you look away, and then you look back. A teenager walks straight past his mother, gets ten steps, then turns and comes back into her arms as if he'd only been pretending not to know her. The boys' father comes through in a wrinkled suit and goes down on one knee, and they hit him at full speed, and he lets them knock him over.

Everyone here is waiting for someone they love.

You're waiting for a stranger.

When you were small your father used to drive you out to the north side of the airport on Saturday mornings, to the observation plaza where the speakers play the tower, and the two of you would sit on the hood of the car and watch them come in two hundred yards away while a calm voice overhead told each one it was cleared to land. He knew the aircraft by their sounds. He'd say *heavy* before you could see it, and he was always right. Years later, when you came home from the year in Singapore with everything you owned in two suitcases, he was at this rail, in a jacket he'd worn for the occasion, and he'd been there an hour because he didn't trust the traffic. He doesn't drive at night anymore. He says it's his eyes. You've never asked him how long he stood here, and he's never said, and you understand now, standing where he stood, that he wouldn't have minded.

You take out your phone. You open a blank note, type IRIS WREN, and make the letters as large as they'll go. Then you hold it up at chest height and feel every bit as ridiculous as you expected to. One of the drivers in the dark suits glances at you, at your phone, and gives you a brief professional nod, one colleague to another.

The doors open. Close. Open. A balloon slips out of somebody's fist and rises to the ceiling, forty feet up, where it stays. Your arm gets tired, and you switch arms, and you find you don't mind any of it. Every time the doors open and someone is found, a little of it reaches you secondhand, a borrowed share of every reunion in the room. By now you've had dozens.
