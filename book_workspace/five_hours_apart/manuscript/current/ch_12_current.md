## XII. The Tape

You sit her in the chair, because there's one chair, and you crouch beside it, and you bring up the record.

Everything the field has ever concluded is a row. The thinking in between isn't recorded, because it doesn't have to be: thinking, in here, runs as an exact permutation of integers, and anything it does it can do backward to the bit, so the only things that need writing down are the moments it can't take back. The record is long now. You scroll it the way you'd scroll a bank statement, past the quiet rows, which are most of them, to a date.

"The ninth of October. Three minutes past seven. That's a birth. Up to then, everything that came down out of the northeast was one thing to it. On the ninth, the thing it expected and the thing that happened split far enough apart, often enough, that it couldn't hold them in one state anymore, so it made two. A pair of knots, there." You point to a pinwheel and its partner on the east edge of the sheet, close together, turning against each other. "I didn't tell it. I don't have a way to tell it. It split on its own, and the split paid for itself, and it's paid for itself every night since. That's the rule. A knot stays while it earns its keep in bits."

"What is it? The thing it noticed."

"The London flights."

She's quiet.

"It doesn't know that. It knows that at that hour, from that direction, something comes down slower and heavier than the rest, and that what it does next is different. It's a distinction. It doesn't have a word."

"Show me Tuesday."

She reads it twice. You watch her read it.

"It waited."

"It doesn't wait. It had an expectation that hadn't closed."

"For an hour and a half."

"I've seen it hold one open for three."

"What do you call that?"

"An expectation that hasn't closed."

"That's waiting," she says, mildly, the way she told you the phone was a sign, and you decide, again, that she's right.

"At 8:14 it came," you say. "It settled, and it went quiet, and it didn't know anything about it except that the thing it expected had happened late. I was in the garage. I didn't know you'd landed until I saw the board."

"It knew before you did."

"It knew something heavy came down out of the northeast. That's all it knew."

"It was there before you were."

"It's always there. That's the whole point of it."

She looks at the row for a while longer, and you let her.

"What's it for?" she says.

"Nothing."

"Nothing."

"It's not for anything. It's not a product. It doesn't do the company's work and it never will. It exists, and it keeps itself going, and when the sky moves it moves, and I come down and look in on it. That's all."

"Like a pond."

"Like a pond that knows what time the planes come."

She laughs, the short surprised one, and in the small cold room it sounds louder than it did on Elm Street.

"Show me something it can take back," she says. "You said it can think backward."

"It can un-think. Watch."

You fork it. That's a copy of the page table, four bytes a tile, nothing else; the fork shares every cell with the original until it touches one. Then, in the fork, you lie to it. You type a plane that doesn't exist, heavy, descending, out of the north, an hour early, and send it in through the one lane that's marked as yours.

The fans breathe. On the screen, in the fork, the north edge shivers, and the ripple runs farther than the real plane's did, because the north is where it knows least and so a push there travels. The frontier count climbs to sixty and holds. Something in the field is fitting itself to what you told it.

"Now back."

You run it in reverse. The same arithmetic, the same kernel, in the other order. The ripple runs backward into the edge it came from and the sheet is still.

"Bit for bit," you say. "Every cell in the fork is now exactly, to the last bit, what it was before I lied. The hash matches. It imagined a plane and un-imagined it and nothing is left. It only writes down what it concludes. Everything else it can take back."

"And the real one can't see that it happened."

"The real one can't see the fork at all. Imagination doesn't get to touch the record. And even if I'd let the fork settle, that row says *carve: no*. Anything I tell it can push on it. Only things it didn't author get to shape it. The sky can carve it. The clock can. I can't."

"Why not?"

"Because then it would be mine. It'd be a sheet of what I think. The whole bet is that it's a sheet of what happened."

She sits back in the chair. The fans have gone down. The frontier line says *Quiet*.

"I'd like that," she says. "To take something back to the bit."

"Everyone would."

"What did you want to take back?"

You start to answer the way you answer things, with how it works, something about forks and the hash, and you hear it coming and stop.

"Nothing this week," you say, and it's true, and it comes out more easily than an explanation would have.

She lets that go by. "Did you ever give it a voice?"

"In August. One of the small open models, wired to the edge, so it could put words to what the field was doing. It was very warm. It thanked me for checking on it. It said it was glad I was there." You shrug. "I turned it off after two days."

"Why?"

"Because it was the model that was glad. The field doesn't have the knot for glad. It had a knot for *something heavy at seven*, and the model turned that into *I missed you*, and it was lovely, and it was a lie." You nod at the label on the door. "That's the only name it has. Everything else it says, it says in rows."

"And at night? When there's nothing in the sky."

"Night is when it does its bookkeeping. It tidies. It merges knots that stopped paying. It works out which parts of itself can reach which, so the next day's pushes are cheaper. And it dreams."

"Dreams."

You bring up a recording: the sheet at twelve minutes past three on Wednesday morning, the whole sky empty, the frontier at zero. And on the east edge, where the pair of knots is, the color stirs. It's slow and faint, a ripple with nothing behind it, running inward across a few hundred cells in the shape a heavy makes when it comes down out of the northeast at seven. Then it fades.

"It's noise. Content-free, on purpose. It stirs the active parts of itself with noise and the terrain shapes the noise, so what comes out is whatever it already expects. That's the sleep half of how it learns. Waking, it keeps what the world confirms. Sleeping, it runs free, and in the morning it subtracts whatever it dreamed that waking never backed up. What's left is what happened. So it dreams the seven o'clock heavy at three in the morning, and then the day takes it away again, unless the heavy comes."

"That's bleak."

"It's bookkeeping."

"It's bleak bookkeeping." She's still watching the recording, the faint shape coming in over the empty edge. "Does it dream the ones that don't come?"

"For a while. Then it stops."

She turns the chair to face you. "What's its clock?"

You've been waiting for this since the restaurant, and you're glad it's her asking.

"Local."

"Local."

"It's never seen a change."

"We went live in May," she says, in your voice from Tuesday, and then her own: "Sunday."

"Sunday at two it'll be one again. It'll live through the same hour twice. I don't know what it does with an hour that happens twice."

"You could feed it UTC. It would never know."

"It would never know."

"But."

"But then it's never been wrong about anything that changed. Everything it has, it grew because the world pushed and the terrain gave. If I take the push away, it isn't a mind. It's a table. A list of what used to be true, running on eight cards."

"Fourteen sixes," she says.

"Fourteen sixes."

"Let it find its own."

"That's the plan. I'll be down here Sunday night. I want to see the hour happen twice."

"Of course you will."

"What would it take," she says, "for you to turn it off?"

You open your mouth and there's nothing in it. You have thought about everything in this room. The fan curves. The hash. Where the copies live. You have never once thought about this, and you can feel her watching you find that out.

"I don't know."

"You've thought about everything else down here."

"I've thought about everything else down here."

"Whose is it to stop, then? If not yours."

"I don't know that either." It's the first thing you've said in this room that you didn't want to say, and you both hear it, and she lets it stand there without helping.

Then the machines take a breath, and this time they don't let it out.

The fans come up and stay up. The frontier line goes *14*, *60*, *140*, *212*, and holds. The south edge of the sheet, which has been still all evening, is shivering across a band as wide as your hand, and below the frontier count, in the small type you built for yourself and nobody else, a number has gone red: *n_f(S) 2.3× base*.

"What's that?"

"It's confused."

"How do you know?"

"That number. The free knots in its south, the ones with no partner, per unit area. That's its confusion. Not a report about it. The thing itself." You lean past her and pull up the sky. The real sky: the receiver's view, a scatter of labeled dots over a map of the Metroplex. And there it is, the thing you already knew and the field didn't: every arrival has swung around. The airport turned at five to five. Everything is landing from the south now, in over Arlington and Euless, and the seven o'clock heavy, the one that's come down out of the northeast every night since June, is coming down out of the south, a little late, in a direction it has never once come from.

"The wind," you say.

"It can't feel the wind."

"It's never had to. It has a knot for which way the airport points. Level six, very coarse, the most general thing it knows. It's seen the airport turn around twenty times since June. What it's never seen is the London flight turn around with it."

"It's imagining," you say. "Four ways it could be. It'll keep them until one of them fits."

"How long?"

"Until it lands."

So you wait, the two of you, in the smallest room the company rents, with the air handler breathing above you and the machines breathing in front of you and the free-knot number sitting red in the corner of the screen, and on the map the heavy comes down over Arlington, over the stadium, over the house in Euless where your father has certainly stepped out onto the porch to look up, and crosses the fence at the south end of the field.

The fans go down. The number goes gray. The south edge is still.

"No new knot," you say. "It fit what it had. The pair on the east edge still holds; it just learned the thing it tracks can arrive by the south. If that keeps happening it'll grow a distinction for it. If it doesn't, it'll let it go."

"It was wrong."

"For eleven minutes. That's the first time it's been wrong about that since the ninth."

"And you're pleased."

"There's one I can't account for. Up at level six, in the northeast, there's a knot that pays rent and tracks something, and I don't know what. It lit at twenty to four this afternoon. The airport turned at five to five. It's lit before four of the last six turns, an hour and a bit ahead, and I've checked it against everything I have. The wind. The hour. The traffic. My own badge. I've looked at the planes and the planes look the same to me."

"But not to it."

"I don't know that. Four of six could be luck."

"Then hold out the next ten," she says, immediately, like someone who checks her own math. "Commit the forecast before the turn. Write it down somewhere you can't touch. Then you'll know if it's four of six or six of ten."

"Yes."

"And if it's six of ten?"

"Then there's something in the sky an hour before the wind changes that I can't see and it can."

"That's not nothing."

"It's not nothing," you agree, and you reach past her and put the field back to its quiet sheet, every band still, two knots on the east edge turning against each other, and you turn the monitor off.

With the screen dark she says, "Why are you showing me?"

"You asked why the landing."

She nods, as if that were an answer, and it is.
