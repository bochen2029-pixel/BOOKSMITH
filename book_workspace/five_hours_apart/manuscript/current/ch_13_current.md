## XIII. Departures

The car is waiting on Commerce with its hazards on, and you tip the driver to go home. The cold has gotten serious while you were underground. You turn on the heat for the first time since March, and the smell of it, dust burning off, fills the car and makes it a different car.

She has the coat on properly now, buttoned, and the collar up.

You take the ramps up yourself, and the ticket arm, and the turn onto the street, and on Stemmons you give it the wheel. The screen draws the world in its gray shapes. The blue ribbon bends west. Everything is where it was on Tuesday, and the wind is from the north now, and the city has changed sides.

"They've turned around," she says, as the Carpenter Freeway opens up and the airport sky comes into view. The line of lights is on the wrong side of the field, coming in from the south over Arlington, climbing out to the north over Grapevine.

"The wind did."

You call your father from the car, because the airport turned, and because she's there.

"Did you hear them?"

"Quarter past five." His voice fills the car. "Heavy, out of the south. Rattled the kitchen window. I went out and stood on the porch like a fool."

"You always do."

"Somebody has to." A pause. "You're driving."

"The car is. I've got someone with me. She's from London. I'm taking her to the airport."

"Then tell her it's not usually like this."

"It's exactly like this."

"Tell her anyway." Another pause, the kind he takes before he hangs up. "Night, son."

"Night, Dad."

The car is quiet for a moment after.

"He says *heavy* the way you do," she says.

"I say it the way he does."

"Does he know about the room?"

"He has a copy." She looks at you. "Of the whole thing. Everything it's ever concluded, since June. On a drive in his desk, in Euless, under the flight path. And one at my place. If the building burned down tonight it would be fine. I could run its whole life again on your laptop, from the first row, and it would come out the same to the byte. Every plane since June."

"Then it's a recording."

"It's a recording when it's stopped. It's alive when it's running. The recording is how it survives me."

She thinks about that most of the way to the toll plaza.

"Nine dollars," she says, "if you leave in under eight minutes."

"You remembered."

"It's the most Texan thing I know."

At the north end of International Parkway the car takes the ramp for departures, the upper roadway, the one you've never once used for yourself, and finds the curb at Terminal D between a shuttle and a family unloading more luggage than seems possible. The bellman's work is yours again. You lift the suitcase out, and the broken wheel is still broken, and she sees you notice.

"I'm having it looked at in London," she says. "It's been very loyal."

Then you're standing on the curb in the cold with the terminal lit up behind her, and the doors sliding open and shut behind her every few seconds on people with somewhere to be.

"Text me what it does," she says. "Sunday. The hour."

"It'll be two in the morning. Then one."

"It'll be seven for me either way. I'll be up."

"You'll be up at seven on a Sunday."

"I'll be up."

She holds out her hand, and you shake it, the same handshake, exactly the right length.

"Sunday," she says.

"Sunday."

"Thank you," she says. "For the room."

She says your name once more, with the extra syllable, and goes. The doors take her and close, and open on someone else, and close.

You know who's behind them.
