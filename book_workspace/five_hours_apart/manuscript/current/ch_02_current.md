## II. Stemmons

Say the message comes on Stemmons.

But first you have to get out of the garage, and that part you do yourself. The car could probably manage it by now. It can back out of a space and find its own way to the street, and people post videos of it threading garages tighter than this one. But there's a difference between what a thing can do and what you'll let it do, and the spiral ramp up from four levels under the street, the ticket arm, and the blind right turn onto Commerce are three things you still like to do with your own hands. You take it up the spiral, past the same concrete pillars with the same scuffs at bumper height, and the arm lifts, and you are up and out in the last of the light.

Downtown at seven belongs to valets and cleaning crews and people walking dogs past dark lobbies. On a patio someone laughs at the end of a story you'll never hear the beginning of. Above the old Magnolia Building the red Pegasus stands with its wings up, as it has, in one form or another, since 1934.

At the first red light you give the car the wheel. When the light changes, the wheel moves under your hands, and you let it, and then you take your hands away. Your job now is to watch, which turns out to be a different thing from driving. You see more.

On the screen beside you, the car redraws the world as it understands it: lanes as clean lines, other cars as smooth gray shapes with no faces and no drivers, a blue ribbon laid out ahead to show where it means to go. Every fraction of a second it imagines the next few seconds a dozen different ways and chooses one. You know roughly how it does this. You've read the papers and argued about the architectures over lunch. Knowing hasn't made it any less strange. It's the closest thing you know to watching a thought.

Stemmons carries you north out of the core. The Design District's warehouses slide by on your left, and beyond them, past the levee, the long dark of the floodway. Ahead the brake lights come on in waves, a mile of red flinching at once, and then let go. In the mirror Reunion's globe gets smaller and keeps glowing.

This is the part of the day that belongs to no one. You're not at work and not yet home, and nothing is asked of you except to sit here and be carried. You have always liked it more than you could explain to anyone.

You had plans tonight, if the kind you make with yourself count. On the desk at home there's a yoke clamped to the edge and a throttle quadrant beside it, and tonight was going to be Heathrow. You've been flying simulators since you were twelve, when the airport was the biggest thing in your world and the game was the only way to get inside it. These days you fly the big jets, and you fly them the way the real pilots mostly don't anymore, by hand, and what you fly is the arrival. Tonight was going to be the long descent over the Channel in the dark and the lights of London coming up under the nose, the river, the towers off the left, twenty-seven left with a crosswind if the weather wanted one. The approach is the only part that's ever interested you. Seven hours of ocean fly themselves.

Then the screen chimes. 7:06. Dana, who runs your group.

> *You still downtown? Iris Wren's flight sat on the ground at Heathrow for two hours. Lands 8:10 now, Terminal D. Her car service gave up and I've got a kid with a fever. Any chance you could grab her? She's at the hotel on Elm, right by the office.*
>
> *And feed her. On us.*

Iris Wren, from the London office, who presents to the review council at nine tomorrow morning, and whom you have never met. To you she's what most people at the company are: a name at the top of documents. Hers are the good kind, careful and a little dry, written by someone who checks her own math. Her profile photo is the size of a postage stamp. You couldn't pick her out of a crowd.

For about three seconds you resist. Heathrow was right there. Then the three seconds pass.

"Hey Grok," you say. The assistant answers in the unhurried voice you chose for it because it was the least eager of the options. "Take me to DFW. Terminal D, arrivals."

The blue ribbon on the screen lifts off the lanes for home, considers, and lays itself down again bending west toward the Carpenter Freeway. The car, which has no opinions about anything, takes the new ribbon without comment. You dictate two words back to Dana, *On it*, and watch the lanes for home continue north without you.

Through Irving the towers of Las Colinas stand lit and mostly empty, their logos glowing for no one. Then the sky ahead changes. Over the airport the dark goes the dull amber of a place that never fully turns off, and there they are, the landing lights you sorted from the forty-first floor, close now. Four, five of them, strung out toward the runways, so evenly spaced they look like one slow thought being thought very carefully. Each of them holds a hundred people, or two hundred, or three. Each of those people is coming from somewhere, and being met, or not.
