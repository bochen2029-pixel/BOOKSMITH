## I. Forty-One

Say it's a Tuesday in the last week of October, the odd week when London sits five hours ahead of Dallas instead of six, though you have no reason yet to know that.

You're on the forty-first floor. Everyone else left an hour ago, and the motion sensors have been putting the office to sleep behind you, one bank of lights at a time, until the only light left on the floor is yours. Somewhere down the hall a vacuum runs, stops, runs again. The dark monitors on the empty desks around you each hold a faint copy of you if you look at them, so you don't.

What the company does, if anyone asks at a party, is move money. What it actually does is move the information that moves money, a few hundred million times a day, and your job is to make sure the models that watch all that information are telling the truth. You build the tests that catch them when they aren't. It's a strange kind of work to explain. Most days you don't try.

What you're looking at is a test. For a week it has passed forty-nine times out of fifty, and the fiftieth time it fails for no reason anyone can find, which means there is a reason and nobody has found it yet. You've carried it to lunch and to the gym and into the shower, where most of your real thinking gets done. You carried it this afternoon through two meetings in which you nodded at the right moments and heard almost nothing.

At 6:52 you find it. Two processes, each convinced it got there first. Almost always, one of them is right. One run in fifty they cross in the dark, and everything downstream falls over in a way that looks like the model's fault and isn't.

The fix is four lines. You write them and run the test fifty times, then two hundred, and watch the results come up green, row after row of identical successes filling the screen like windows coming on in a tower at dusk.

Nobody will ever know which four lines. Sometime tomorrow someone will notice the test has stopped failing and assume it fixed itself, as people assume the lights come on by themselves. That's fine. This is the part you would do for free. It's the part you would do if no one paid you and no one ever found out: the moment something that was wrong becomes, under your hands, simply true.

You lean back, and the chair creaks, and you look out the window for the first time in hours.

The city has come on while you weren't watching. The freeways run white toward you and red away, braided where they cross, never quite still. West of downtown the Trinity is a long band of nothing, the one dark thing in the frame. Reunion Tower's globe cycles through its slow patterns of light. And farther out, past all of it, the landing lights hang in the dark in their patient lines.

Two lines, in fact. You can tell them apart without trying. The low ones, close and frequent, are Love Field's: 737s coming down over Bachman Lake every couple of minutes, the way they have your whole life. The high ones, far out past Irving, slow and evenly spaced, are DFW's. South wind tonight, so they're landing on the seventeens and eighteens, coming in over Grapevine. You know this the way you know your own address. You grew up in Euless, in the Mid-Cities, under the airport, and the sound of a heavy on final at three thousand feet is the closest thing to a lullaby you've ever had.

You've watched those lights from this window for years. You have never once wondered who was on them.

On the windowsill a black receiver the size of a thumb blinks once a second, as it has since June, listening to a sky it can't see. You don't look at it.

You pack the laptop. You find your badge. The elevator takes you down forty-one floors without a sound, and halfway there your ears do the small thing they do at the end of a flight.

P4 is four levels under the street, the bottom of the garage, where there's always a space and the air never moves. It smells like concrete and cooling engines. Your car sees you coming and unlocks, and its mirrors unfold like something waking up. Before you've touched anything, the screen has guessed: HOME, in white letters on the map, because it's after six on a weekday and it has learned what you do after six on a weekday. Twenty-five minutes, it says, straight up Stemmons, and it's usually right.
