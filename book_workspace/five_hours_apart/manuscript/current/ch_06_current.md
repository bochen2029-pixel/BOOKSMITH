## VI. Forty-Eight

The elevator is mirrored and very fast. Your ears do the thing again, in reverse. Less than three hours ago you came down a tower four blocks from here, alone.

The restaurant is nearly empty at this hour, which makes it feel as though it's been kept open for you: low light, dark wood, white linen, and glass from floor to ceiling. The host walks you to a table against the window, and the city lays itself out below you to its edges. There are the freeways you drove tonight, braided and moving, and the black band of the Trinity. Far off to the west is the faint amber of the airport, where an hour ago you stood with your arm in the air.

Below, a couple of blocks off, the red Pegasus stands on its roof with its wings up. You've looked up at that horse your whole life. This is the first time you've looked down at it.

"Is that a horse?" she asks. "With wings?"

"Pegasus. An oil company put it up there in 1934. It was the tallest thing in town. Pilots used it to find the city at night."

"It's still there."

"That one's a copy. The original rusted out, and they took it down and put that up, and then they found the original in a shed and fixed it, and now it's on the ground in front of a hotel by the convention center, where you can walk right up to it."

"Why a flying horse, though?"

You open your mouth and find you have nothing. "I know everything about it except that."

She seems to like this better than an answer would have been.

In the window her reflection sits over the city, a little transparent, the lights of downtown showing through it, and for a moment that's what you're looking at instead of the skyline. Then the waiter arrives, and you look at the menu.

You talk like two people who have a company in common and nothing else yet: Dana, the London office, the weather, the flight, the specific misery of the middle seat, which she didn't have this time but has had before and has opinions about. By the time the bread arrives, the talk has found its footing. She grew up in Leeds and has lived in London twelve years and still gets lost south of the river, which she says is a London tradition. She asks how long you've been in Dallas.

"Always. Give or take Singapore."

"That's rare, isn't it? In your line of work."

"Everyone I work with is from somewhere else. It's like being the only person at the party who lives in the house."

"Is that lonely?"

You tell her about the sensors. How the floor goes dark behind you one bank at a time, and how there's a setting for it somewhere, and how you've never once asked anyone to change the setting. It's a good answer to a question she didn't ask.

"That's how the lights work," she says.

"That's how the lights work."

"I asked about you."

"I know." You turn your glass. "It's something. I'm not sure it's that."

Her phone lights up on the tablecloth. She glances at it, and something in her face closes a little.

"Sorry," she says. "Tomorrow."

"The presentation."

"The presentation," she says, "has a hole in it, and I've been staring into the hole since Sunday."

She tells you. Her team runs a model that watches the transfers moving between the London systems and yours. Since Sunday night it has flagged the same hour every night: midnight to one, London time. "It's the quietest hour we have," she says, "and since Sunday it looks like rush hour." Nothing shipped over the weekend. Nothing changed. She has retrained the model, checked the features, checked the data twice. "I checked it again on the plane, which is a very sad sentence to say out loud." She turns her glass a quarter turn on the linen. "Go on, then. Your whole job is knowing when models are wrong. What would you check?"

"When did it start?"

"Sunday night."

"What else happened Sunday?"

She frowns. "Nothing. It was Sunday. I did laundry."

And there it is: the same click you felt at your desk at 6:52, the quiet sound a problem makes when it rolls over and shows you its underside. Except this time there's someone across the table to hear it.

"Your clocks went back on Sunday," you say.

She opens her mouth, and closes it.

"Ours don't go back until next Sunday. This week you're five hours ahead of us, not six. If anything in that pipeline converts our time to yours by adding six—"

"—then it isn't midnight," she says slowly. "It's eleven."

"It's eleven. And eleven is busy."

"So it isn't wrong about the traffic."

"It isn't wrong about the traffic. It's wrong about the time."

She puts both hands flat on the tablecloth, as if steadying the table. "We went live in May."

"So it's never seen a clock change."

"It has never," she says, "seen a clock change." Then she laughs, a real one this time, helpless and a little appalled, loud enough that a couple two tables over look up from their dessert.

You watch the week leave her. It goes out of her shoulders first, then her jaw, then the faint crease between her eyebrows that you'd assumed was just her face. What you feel, watching it go, isn't quite pride, and it isn't quite the click from your desk either. It's warmer than that, and it's secondhand. It reaches you through her, as the old man's face at the arrivals doors reached you, and it's out of all proportion to what you actually did, which was ask her what day it was.

"The fix is one line," she says.

"The fix is one line. Finding every place somebody wrote a six is the rest of it."

"I present at nine."

You hear yourself say it before you've decided to. "I'll be in at seven. We'll find the sixes."

"You don't have to do that."

"I know."

You'll need to be up at five-thirty. You're already a little sorry about it, as you're always a little sorry about the needle when you give blood: a small, sharp cost, paid on purpose, for a feeling you've never managed to put into words and have stopped trying to.

The plates come and go. Somewhere in there the conversation stops being about the company at all, and neither of you notices the moment it happens.

"You said something in the car," she says. "That you trust it about as much as anything you mostly understand. What's the part you don't?"

"Whether anything's going on in there."

"Going on."

"Everyone I work with wants to know what it can do. Whether it's right, whether it's safe, whether it's cheaper than the last one. Those are the questions I get paid for. The one I don't get paid for is whether anything is like anything for it. When it gets something wrong, is that anything? When the elevator drops, do its ears pop?"

"Do you think they do?"

"Not the ones we build. Not yet. But I don't think it's the material. I think it's the shape. I think if you got the processing right, the kind of thing it's doing to itself, it'd be there, in silicon or in meat or in a sheet of anything, and it wouldn't care what it was running on." You hear your own voice and bring it down. "And you'd never know. That's the part. You could build it and sit across a table from it and never know."

"Would it matter? If you couldn't tell."

"It'd matter to it."

She doesn't say anything for a moment, and you have time to hear what you've just done, which is give a speech at a dinner table to a woman who has been awake for twenty-four hours.

"Sorry. That's the speech I give nobody."

"I noticed you had it ready."

"Since about 2019."

She laughs, and it isn't the polite one. And there's a pleasure in this that isn't the fix and isn't the view and isn't the thing you noticed at the doors and looked away from. It's the pleasure of handing someone the thing you care about and watching them hold it properly. You'd forgotten it was on the list.

"What would you have been doing tonight?" she asks. "If Dana hadn't texted."

"Honestly?"

"Honestly."

"I'd have flown to London."

She waits.

"In a simulator. On the computer at home. It's what I do instead of television. Tonight was going to be the approach into Heathrow."

"You were going to land at Heathrow tonight."

"Twenty-seven left. There's usually a westerly. You come in over the city—"

"I know how you come in. I've done it a hundred times."

"From the inside."

"From the inside. You see a seatback and a man's elbow."

"From the front it's the best ten minutes there is. Seven hours of nothing, and then the whole city comes up out of the dark and you've got ten minutes not to ruin it."

"Why the landing, though? Of all of it."

You think about it, because she asked it like she wanted the real answer. "The cruise flies itself. Nobody's needed. The landing is the only part where it matters that there's someone in the seat."

She looks at you for a moment longer than the sentence needed.

"So you've seen my city from the front a hundred times," she says, "and never once been in it."

"And you've been in it twelve years and never once seen it from the front."

Neither of you says anything for a while after that, and it isn't awkward, which surprises you. Below the window a train slides along Pacific Avenue, lit and empty. Out west the amber over the airport hasn't changed. It won't.

"It's four in the morning in me," she says eventually. "Do you know the only thing I like about jet lag? For a day or so you're not anywhere. You're between."

"It's that week for everybody. Your clocks changed and ours haven't."

"Five hours instead of six."

"Until Sunday."

"Until Sunday," she agrees, and looks back out at the city, and that is all either of you says about it.

The check comes, and you hand over the company card, as instructed. By the time the waiter brings it back it's after eleven here, which makes it after four in her body, and it's beginning to show. She's still bright, but bright like a phone at two percent.

"I won't sleep," she says. "Not for an hour at least. I know how this goes." She looks down at the window. "Could I see the horse? From underneath?"
