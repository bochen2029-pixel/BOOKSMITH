## VII. Elm Street

Downtown at a quarter past eleven on a Tuesday is a stage after the play. The streets are warm and nearly empty, and the stoplights go through their changes for nobody. A man hoses down a patio, and the smell of wet concrete comes across the street to meet you. A security guard in a lobby lifts two fingers from his desk as you pass, and you lift two back.

She walks with her coat over her arm, still, as if she's been carrying it so long she's forgotten it's there. She walks the way she came through the doors: reading everything.

"Is it always this empty?"

"At night. In the daytime it's hotter."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one anybody from here ever gives."

A train comes along Pacific as you cross it, two cars, lit the way a kitchen is lit at three in the morning, with no one on it at all, and slides away toward the West End.

"Would you ever leave?" she asks. "Dallas."

"I think about it the way I think about learning the piano."

"Which is?"

"Fondly. Every few years."

She laughs, and the laugh goes up the empty street and doesn't come back.

Then you're under it. From the forty-eighth floor it was a toy, something you could have picked up. From the street it's forty feet of red neon on a steel derrick, turning so slowly you have to watch a while to be sure, two horses back to back so that whichever way you're facing there's one coming toward you. The red gets on everything: the sidewalk, the windows across the street, her face when she tips it back to look.

"It's bigger from down here."

"Most things are."

"It's going round."

"It's always gone round."

She watches it make most of a turn. "I'd have used it too," she says. "If I were flying in. I'd have aimed for that."

Back at the hotel the doors are the revolving kind, and the lobby beyond them is all marble and hush. She turns and offers her hand, and you shake it, a handshake of exactly the right length.

"Seven," she says.

"Seven."

"Thank you," she says. "For the sign."

The door turns, and takes her, and turns again empty. All night you watched doors open and close on people you didn't know. This time you know who's behind them.
