# Back-translation (de-DE → EN), unit: ch_04 — Half A, blind

### ch_04.001
## IV. The Doors

### ch_04.002
Let's say the doors release her [ambiguous: "sie" = her or them; context: her] at four minutes past nine.

### ch_04.003
You do not recognize her. There is nothing to recognize; a postage stamp is not a face. What you recognize is the way she comes out, and that is the way she writes. She is not looking for anyone. She looks at everything, reads the hall from left to right, as if it [ambiguous: "sie" = the hall (Halle) or she herself] were a page that she wants to read properly. Over one arm she carries a folded wool coat, a London coat, wrong for here, and she pulls a suitcase behind her that tips to the side on a broken wheel.

### ch_04.004
You register that she is beautiful, the way your ears registered [perfect: "have registered"] the descent from the forty-first floor: not as a thought, only as a change that your body notices before you have decided anything about it. Then you look at the suitcase.

### ch_04.005
Her eyes find the phone. She stops in front of you, reads her own name and looks up.

### ch_04.006
"That's me," she says. "I've never had a sign."

### ch_04.007
"That's a phone."

### ch_04.008
"That's a sign," she says, gently, like someone who is correcting a definition in a draft, and you decide that she is right.

### ch_04.009
You tell her your name. She repeats it carefully, like someone who wants to keep it [behalten = keep / remember]. Her accent is something from the North that twelve years of London have flattened but not quite wiped out, and she pronounces your name as if it had one syllable more than you have given it so far.

### ch_04.010
"Dana said you had volunteered."

### ch_04.011
"Dana volunteered me."

### ch_04.012
"Then thank you for having been volunteered." A smile, there and gone again. "And I'm so sorry. You must have been here an hour."

### ch_04.013
"Roughly."

### ch_04.014
"How awful."

### ch_04.015
"It wasn't," you say, and you mean it, and you cannot explain why without sounding strange, so you leave it.

### ch_04.016
You reach for the suitcase. She says it's fine [lit. "that goes already" = I can manage], it's broken, she's embarrassed. You say you'll take it. There follows one more round of the old ritual in which two strangers decline and accept the same favor, and then she lets you take it, and you (pl.) both walk across the pedestrian bridge to the parking garage, the broken wheel ticking behind you (pl.) over every joint in the floor.

### ch_04.017
The parking garage is open toward the night, and the air that draws through it is seventy-one degrees Fahrenheit and smells of kerosene [Kerosin = kerosene / jet fuel] and freshly mown grass. She stops.

### ch_04.018
"It's warm," she says, as if she were reporting a defect. "It's the end of October."

### ch_04.019
"It's the end of October in Texas."

### ch_04.020
She looks down at the coat over her arm as if it had personally disappointed her.

#### notes ch_04
- "als wäre sie eine Seite, die sie ordentlich lesen will": the two "sie" can both point to Iris, so the image (the hall as a page) can blur into "as if she were a page".
- "Sagen wir, die Türen geben sie ... frei": "sie" could be read as "them" for a beat; context settles it as "her".
- "Dann danke, dass du freiwillig gemeldet worden bist": the passive joke lands, but the construction is clunky in German (presumably deliberate).
- "wie jemand, der ihn behalten will": "behalten" = keep or remember; both readings work.
- Iris and the narrator use "du" from the first line; plausible for colleagues, no formal "Sie" anywhere.
