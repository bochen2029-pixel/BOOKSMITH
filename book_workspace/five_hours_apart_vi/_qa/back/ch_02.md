# Back-translation (vi-VN → English), blind, half A: ch_02

### ch_02.001
## II. Stemmons

### ch_02.002
Just say [or: let us suppose] the message comes while he is on Stemmons.

### ch_02.003
But first he has to get out of the parking garage, and that part he does himself. The car by now could probably manage on its own. It can back itself out of the space and find its own way out to the street, and someone has even posted video of it weaving through parking garages tighter than this one. But between what a thing can do and what he allows it to do there is a difference, and the spiral ramp going up from four levels below the street, the ticket-checking barrier, the blind right turn onto Commerce Street are three things he still likes to do with his own hands. He takes the car up the spiral, past the familiar concrete pillars with the familiar scrapes at bumper height, then the barrier lifts, and he has made it up, outside, into a little of the day's last light.

### ch_02.004
Downtown [lit. "the city center"] at seven o'clock belongs to the parking attendants, the cleaning crews, and the people walking dogs past the darkened lobbies. On the porch [patio] of a restaurant, someone laughs at the end of a story whose beginning he will never get to hear. On the roof of the old Magnolia building, the red Pegasus stands with its wings raised, as it has always stood, in this form or another, since 1934.

### ch_02.005
At the first red light, he hands the driving [lit. "the steering hand"] over to the car. When the light changes, the steering wheel turns by itself under his hands, he lets it be, then draws his hands back. Now his job is to watch, which turns out to be quite different from driving. He gets to see more.

### ch_02.006
On the screen beside him, the car redraws the world the way it understands it: the lanes become neat lines, the other cars become smooth gray blocks with no faces, no drivers, a green [xanh: green or blue] band laid out ahead to show where it means to go. Every tick it pictures the next few seconds in a dozen different ways and then picks one. He knows roughly how it does that. He has read the scientific papers and argued about model architecture over lunch. Knowing does not make it one bit less strange. It is the closest thing he knows to seeing a thought.

### ch_02.007
Stemmons takes him north, out of the city's core. The warehouses of the Design District drift by on the left, and farther, beyond the levee, is the long dark strip of the floodway [lit. "flood-drainage channel"]. Ahead, brake lights come on in waves, a whole mile of red flinching all at once, then letting go. In the mirror, the ball of Reunion Tower grows smaller but is still lit.

### ch_02.008
This is the stretch of the day that belongs to no one. He is no longer at work and not yet home, and nobody asks anything of him except to sit here and be driven. He has always liked this stretch more than he could explain to anyone.

### ch_02.009
Tonight he had plans, if the plans people make with themselves count too. On the desk at home there is a control stick [yoke] clamped to the edge of the desk and a throttle cluster beside it, and tonight was going to be Heathrow. He has played flight simulators since he was twelve, when the airport was the biggest thing in his world and the game was the only way to step inside it. Now he flies the big jets, and flies them the way most real pilots no longer do, by hand, and the thing he flies is the arrival leg. Tonight was going to be the long descent over the English Channel [lit. "the Manche strait"] in the dark, and the lights of London rising under the nose of the plane, the river, the towers on the left, runway two-seven left with a crosswind if the weather wants it that way. The approach leg is the only part that has ever excited him. The seven hours over the ocean fly themselves [or: (the plane) flies itself for those seven hours].

### ch_02.010

### ch_02.012
Iris Wren, of the London office, the person who will present before the review board [lit. "appraisal council"] at nine o'clock tomorrow morning, and someone he has never met. To him, she is what most people in the company still are: a name at the top of documents. Her documents are the good kind, careful and a little dry, written by someone who still checks her own calculations. Her profile picture is only the size of a postage stamp. Put her in a crowd and he could not pick her out.

### ch_02.013
For about three seconds he still holds out. Heathrow is right there. Then the three seconds pass.

### ch_02.014
—Hey Grok, —he says. The assistant answers in the unhurried voice he chose for it because it was the least eager voice among the choices. —Take me to DFW. Terminal D, arrivals hall.

### ch_02.015
The green band on the screen lifts off the lanes toward home, considers, then lays itself down again, curving west, toward the Carpenter Freeway. The car, which never has an opinion about anything, takes the new band without comment. He dictates [lit. "reads for the machine to record"] two words back to Dana, *Leave it to me* [lit. "Let me"], then watches the lanes toward home keep running north without him.

### ch_02.016
Past Irving, the towers of Las Colinas stand lit and nearly empty, their logos shining for nobody at all. Then the sky ahead changes. Over the airport, the dark shifts to the murky amber of a place that never fully switches off, and there they are, the landing lights he sorted from the forty-first floor, close now. Four, five of them, strung into a chain toward the runways, spaced so evenly that they look like a slow thought being thought very carefully. Each one carries a hundred people, two hundred, or three hundred. Each person among them is arriving from somewhere, and has someone meeting them, or not.

#### notes ch_02
- "Giờ hạ cánh lúc 8:10" is ambiguous: "giờ" reads as "landing time" or as "now" ("now landing at 8:10").
- "Anh đọc cho máy ghi hai chữ" ("he reads for the machine to record") is a slightly roundabout way to say "dictates"; "máy ghi" can suggest a tape recorder.
- "*Để tôi*" is two words in Vietnamese, so "hai chữ" holds; in English it comes out as "Let me" / "Leave it to me".
