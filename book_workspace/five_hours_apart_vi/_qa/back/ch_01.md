# Back-translation (vi-VN → English), blind, half A: ch_01

### ch_01.001
## I. Forty-one

### ch_01.002
Just say [or: let us suppose] that it is a Tuesday in the last week of October, the skewed week [or: the off-kilter week], when London goes ahead of Dallas by five hours instead of six, though he has no reason yet to know that.

### ch_01.003
He is on the forty-first floor. Everyone went home an hour ago, and the motion sensors have one after another lulled the office to sleep behind his back, one row of lights at a time, until the only light left on the whole floor is his. Somewhere at the end of the corridor, a vacuum cleaner runs, stops, then runs again. The dark screens on the empty desks around him, each one holds a faint copy of him if he looks into it, so he does not look.

### ch_01.004
What the company does, if anyone asks at a party, is move money. What it really does is move the information that makes money shift, several hundred million times a day, and his job is to make sure the models that keep watch over all that information tell the truth. He writes the tests that catch them red-handed when they do not tell the truth. It is a kind of work that is hard to explain. Most days he does not bother to try.

### ch_01.005
The thing he is looking at is a test. For a whole week now it has passed forty-nine times out of fifty, while the fiftieth time it fails and nobody finds the reason, which means there is a reason and nobody has found it yet. He has carried it with him to lunch, to the gym, even into the bathroom [or: the shower; "phòng tắm"], where most of his real thinking takes place. This afternoon he carried it through two meetings, in which he nodded at the right moments while hearing almost nothing.

### ch_01.006
At 6:52 he finds it. Two processes, each process sure that it arrives first. Almost always one of the two is right. Once in every fifty runs they collide with each other in the dark, and everything behind them collapses in a way that looks like the model's fault but actually is not.

### ch_01.007
The fix is four lines. He writes them then runs the test fifty times, then two hundred times, and watches the results come up green [xanh: green or blue], line joining line, the identical successes covering the screen like windows lighting up one after another on a tower at dusk.

### ch_01.008
No one will know which four lines they are. Tomorrow, sometime, someone will notice that the test no longer fails and assume it got better by itself [lit. "healed on its own"], as people always assume lights turn themselves on. That is fine too. This is the part he is willing to do for nothing. It is the part he would still do even if no one paid and no one knew: the moment a thing that used to be wrong becomes, under his hand, simply right.

### ch_01.009
He leans back, the chair creaks, and for the first time in many hours he looks out the window.

### ch_01.010
The city has lit up while he was not paying attention. The freeways white toward him and red in the opposite direction, braided into each other at the crossings, never entirely standing still. West of downtown [lit. "the center"], the Trinity River is a long strip of nothingness, the only dark thing in the frame. The ball [sphere] of Reunion Tower slowly changes through its patterns of light. And farther still, beyond it all, the landing lights hang suspended in the dark in patient rows.

### ch_01.011
Actually it is two rows. He tells them apart without needing to try. The low row, near and thick, is Love Field's: the 737s coming down over Bachman Lake one every few minutes, as they have done his whole life. The high row, far off beyond Irving, slow and evenly spaced, is DFW's. Tonight the wind is from the south, so they land on runways seventeen and eighteen, flying in from the Grapevine side. He knows this the way he knows his home address. He grew up in Euless, the Mid-Cities area, right under the airport, and the sound of a heavy [lit. "a heavy-class one"] entering the final leg at an altitude of three thousand feet is the thing closest to a lullaby that he ever had.

### ch_01.012
For many years he has watched those lights from this window. Not once has he asked himself who is up there.

### ch_01.013
On the windowsill, a black receiver the size of a thumb blinks once every second, as it has since June, listening to a sky it cannot see. He does not look at it.

### ch_01.014
He puts away the laptop. He looks for [or: finds] his employee badge. The elevator takes him down forty-one floors without a sound, and halfway down his ears do the little thing they always do at the end of a flight.

### ch_01.015
P4 lies four levels below the street, the bottom of the parking garage, where there is always space and the air never stirs. It smells of concrete here, and of engines cooling. The car sees him coming and unlocks, the rearview [side] mirrors springing out like something just waking up. He has not touched anything yet, and the screen has already guessed: HOME, white letters on the map, because it is already past six o'clock on a weekday and it has learned what he does after six on weekdays. Twenty-five minutes, it says, straight along Stemmons, and usually it is right.

#### notes ch_01
- "cái tuần lệch" ("the skewed week") is a coined phrase; a native reader gets "the week that is out of step" only from the clause that follows.
- "dòng này nối dòng kia những lần thành công giống hệt nhau phủ kín màn hình" runs two phrases together with no comma; it is hard to parse on first read (wants a comma after "dòng kia").
- "Anh chưa chạm vào gì, màn hình đã đoán trước" is a comma splice; it reads fine as colloquial "he hadn't touched anything and the screen had already guessed".
