## XI. The smallest room

### ch_11.002
He leads her across the bottom of the parking garage, passing his own car, which unlocks as he goes by because it thinks he has come to take it, then locks again when it sees he hasn't [lit. when it sees not so]. In the far wall there is a door marked for staff [lit. reserved for employees], and after that is a corridor nobody has repainted since the building was still new, lit by the kind of tube lights that buzz. It is longer than it ought to be long, passing doors bearing the names of tenants who moved out many years ago. Halfway along it turns sharply, and at the bend there is a steel door welded shut, on which is mounted a sign saying that the tunnels are closed. The city center used to be connected together underneath, a city beneath the belly of the city where people went from one building to another all summer without ever seeing the sun, then the city decided it wanted people up on the street surface and bricked most of it up. The air down here still moves as if it still remembers somewhere to go.

### ch_11.009
The room is colder than the corridor and much quieter than she imagined; he sees clearly that she waits for a roar and gets nothing. It is about the size of a hotel bathroom. There is a low machine rack holding two machines, each as tall as a bread box, each with four cards plugged in, between them a bundle of cables that he himself routed neatly on a Saturday, together with a few zip ties and an episode of a podcast. There is a folding table, a chair, a monitor, a keyboard, a heater that he has never once turned on, and, taped to the wall above the monitor, a sheet of paper with a column of numbers in his handwriting that he has not updated since August. The ventilation system in the ceiling breathes slowly. As for the machines, at this moment, they do not.

### ch_11.012

### ch_11.013
It takes her a while before she can see it, the way it takes a while to make out a stereogram [ảnh lập thể = stereoscopic image]. It is a field made of tiny squares, millions of squares, each square one single flat color, and the colors flow into each other into bands and slow swirls, red to orange to green to blue then back to red, like an oil sheen on water that has stopped drifting [ambiguous: the sheen has stopped drifting / the water has stopped flowing]. Here and there the bands tighten into a point where all the colors meet at the same moment, a pinwheel the size of a fingernail, and almost every pinwheel has a twin [sóng đôi = paired, side by side] a short distance away, turning in the opposite direction.

### ch_11.014
—What am I looking at here?

### ch_11.015
—Each of those little squares is a cell of the field. Each cell holds an angle, like a compass needle. The color is the direction it is pointing. Cells next to each other want to point the same direction, that is why there are bands. The pinwheels are the places where they can't be made to agree with each other. Those are the only things in it that cannot drift. They can be born, or can die, and only in pairs. Those are its concepts.

### ch_11.017

### ch_11.019

### ch_11.020

There is a loop that keeps each region of it always just inside the edge, and that loop is the only part I wrote that I dare call clever. The rest is physics from the seventies.
