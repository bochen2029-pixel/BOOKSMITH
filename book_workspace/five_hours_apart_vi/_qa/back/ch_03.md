# Back-translation (vi-VN → English), blind, half A: ch_03

### ch_03.001
## III. International Parkway

### ch_03.002
Just say [or: let us suppose] it is eight minutes to eight [lit. "eight o'clock less eight minutes"], when he goes through the south toll plaza and heads down International Parkway.

### ch_03.003
This airport is bigger than even Manhattan. All his life he has known that as a scrap of trivia, and now he feels it, with the road running straight through the middle and the terminals curving out on both sides, each terminal a long lit crescent moon, like ships moored along a dark canal. The people who built them in the shape of half circles, so that the walk from car to boarding gate would be really short, assumed that everyone would forever drive there. He knows this place the way you know a house you grew up in. He knows the train runs inside the security area while the buses run outside, knows the parking lots sit inside the hollow of the crescents, knows Terminal D is on the left going north, knows the waiting lot is full before eight on Friday nights. Beyond the terminals, the planes taxi across the apron [lit. "the yard surface"] slowly and cautiously, as if the ground were deep water.

### ch_03.004
He leaves the car on the fourth level of the Terminal D parking garage, between a minivan and a black sedan whose driver is asleep, seat reclined all the way back, then he goes in.

### ch_03.005
Inside it is the hour when an airport stops performing. The shops are pulling down their roll-up shutters. A man drives a floor-polishing machine in slow circles on the terrazzo [lit. "ground stone"] floor. The announcements thin out and sound as if they are talking to an empty room. On the information board, beside the line LONDON HEATHROW, the word DELAYED blinks and then changes to LANDED 8:14, and a while later still, AT GATE [lit. "HAS ENTERED THE GATE"].

### ch_03.006
She must have flown in on a 777, the big kind, the kind with swept-back wingtips, and from here he knows the whole choreography by heart. The walk out of the jet bridge [lit. "the telescoping tube"]. The lines of people at the passport control desks, short at this hour but never quite empty. The wait at the baggage carousel. Then the frosted glass doors. Forty minutes if everything goes smoothly, and not once has he seen everything go smoothly.

### ch_03.007
So he has forty minutes, and a long day behind him, and he finds the row of vending machines below the baggage claim area, glowing blue [xanh: blue or green] in a wall alcove like a small aquarium. He presses the button for the orange can, Ultra Sunrise, and it drops with the familiar heavy, hollow sound, so cold that it makes his hand ache a little. He pops the tab right there, standing up. The hiss. The first long swallow, orange-lemon [citrus] flavored, cold, and with a faint pleasant chemical taste, like something cooked up in a laboratory by people who are fond of him. Three minutes later it rises behind his eyes, a lift, a brightness, as if someone had just turned up the gain on the whole terminal. He was not tired at all, and now he is even less tired than that. It is a small pleasure, cheap, and completely reliable.

### ch_03.008
International passengers come out on the lower level, through a pair of frosted glass doors that every minute or two slide open, let out a few people squinting at the lights, then close. In front of the doors, behind a railing, a crowd has gathered. There are drivers in dark suits holding tablets that show passengers' names. There is a family holding a homemade sign made of purple tinsel [or: glitter], an old man in an ironed shirt holding a supermarket bouquet still in its plastic sleeve, and two little boys taking turns climbing onto the railing until their mother stops them, and then they start again.

### ch_03.009
Each time the door opens, the whole crowd leans forward again. Each time it happens, someone's face changes.

### ch_03.010
A young woman steps out, and the old man with the flowers lets out a sound he was not meant to hear, and he looks away, then looks back again. A teenage boy walks straight past his mother, gets ten steps and then turns back and drops into her arms as if all this time he had only been pretending not to know her. The two little boys' father comes out in a rumpled suit and goes down on one knee, the two of them rush at him at full speed, and he lets them knock him over.

### ch_03.011
Everyone here is waiting for someone they love.

### ch_03.012
And he is waiting for a stranger.

### ch_03.013
When he was small, on Saturday mornings his father would often drive him out to the north side of the airport, to the plane-watching plaza where loudspeakers played the air traffic control radio, and father and son sat on the hood of the car watching them land two hundred yards [lit. "English yards"] away while a calm voice in the air told each one that it was cleared to land. Dad recognized planes by their sound. Dad said *heavy* before he could see it, and every time Dad was right. Many years later, when he came home after a year in Singapore with his whole fortune in two suitcases, Dad stood at this very railing, in the jacket [áo vest: suit jacket / blazer] he had put on especially for the occasion, and had stood there an hour because Dad did not trust the traffic. Dad does not drive at night anymore. Dad says it is his eyes. He has never asked Dad how long he stood here, and Dad has never said, and now, standing where Dad once stood, he understands that Dad would not have minded at all.

### ch_03.014
He takes out his phone. He opens a blank note, types IRIS WREN, and blows the letters up as big as they go. Then he holds it up at chest height and feels exactly as ridiculous as he had guessed he would. One of the drivers in dark suits glances at him, at his phone, then gives him a short, thoroughly professional nod, as between two people in the same trade.

### ch_03.015
The door opens. Closes. Opens. A balloon slips out of someone's hand and floats up to the ceiling, forty feet high, then rests there. His arm tired, he switches hands, and he realizes that he does not mind this at all. Each time the door opens and someone is found, a little of it comes to him secondhand, a share borrowed from every reunion in the hall. By now he has dozens of shares like that.

#### notes ch_03
- "xuôi xuống International Parkway" ("goes down") sits a little oddly with the later "nhà ga D nằm bên trái khi đi lên phía bắc" (going north); a reader may take "xuôi xuống" as heading south.
- "Anh đâu có mệt, và giờ anh còn bớt mệt hơn thế" ("he was not tired at all, and now even less tired") is a deliberate paradox, but read cold it looks like a slip.
- "áo vest" for Dad's garment reads as a suit jacket / blazer to a Vietnamese reader, not a waistcoat.
