# Back-translation (vi-VN → English), blind, half A: ch_04

### ch_04.001
## IV. The doors

### ch_04.002
Just say [or: let us suppose] the doors open to reveal her at four minutes past nine [lit. "nine o'clock four minutes"].

### ch_04.003
He does not recognize her. There is nothing to recognize; a stamp is not a face. What he recognizes [or: notices; "nhận ra" = recognize / notice] is the way she walks out, which is the very same as the way she writes. She is not looking for anyone. She is looking at everything, reading the whole hall from left to right as if it were a page she means to read properly. She has a wool coat draped over her arm, a London coat, out of place here, and pulls a suitcase tilted all the way to one side on a broken wheel.

### ch_04.004
He registers that she is beautiful, the way his ears registered the ride down from the forty-first floor: not as a thought, only as a change his body perceives before he has time to decide anything about it. Then he looks at the suitcase.

### ch_04.005
Her eyes find the phone. She stops in front of him, reads her name, then looks up.

### ch_04.006
—Here I am [lit. "I here"], —she says. —No one has ever held up a sign to meet me.

### ch_04.007
—That is a phone [lit. "That is the phone"].

### ch_04.008
—That is a sign, —she says, gently, like someone correcting a definition in a draft, and he decides she is right.

### ch_04.009
He says his name. She repeats it very carefully, like someone who intends to remember it. Her voice is a northern English accent that twelve years in London have flattened but not wiped out entirely, and she pronounces his name as if it had one more syllable than the way he usually says it.

### ch_04.010
—Dana says you volunteered.

### ch_04.011
—Dana volunteered on my behalf.

### ch_04.012
—Then thank you for being volunteered on your behalf. —A smile, flickering on then off. —And I am so sorry. You must have been standing here a whole hour already.

### ch_04.013
—About that.

### ch_04.014
—That's awful [lit. "So bad"].

### ch_04.015
—Not at all, —he says, and he means it [lit. "he speaks truly"], and he cannot explain why without it sounding strange, so he does not explain.

### ch_04.016
He reaches for the suitcase. She says it's fine, it's broken, she would feel bad [ngại: embarrassed / reluctant to impose]. He says just let him [ambiguous: "cứ để anh" = "just leave it to me" in his own words, or "leave it to him" as reported speech]. One more round of the old ritual, in which two strangers refuse and then accept the same favor, and then she lets him carry it, and the two of them walk across the connecting bridge to the parking garage, the broken wheel clattering behind them over each joint in the floor.

### ch_04.017
The parking garage opens onto the night, and the air blowing through it is seventy-one degrees F, smelling of jet fuel and freshly cut grass. She stops walking.

### ch_04.018
—It's warm, —she says, like someone reporting a fault [or: an incident]. —It is the end of October.

### ch_04.019
—It is the end of October in Texas.

### ch_04.020
She looks down at the coat draped over her arm as if it were the one that had let her down.

#### notes ch_04
- "Anh bảo cứ để anh" leaves "anh" pointing two ways: his own first-person "let me" (as he would say it to her) or narrator's "leave it to him".
- "nhận ra" is used for both "recognize" and "notice" in ch_04.003 ("Anh không nhận ra cô… Thứ anh nhận ra là cách cô bước ra"); the play works in Vietnamese but the two senses blur.
- "Cô bảo không sao đâu, nó hỏng rồi, ngại lắm" is indirect speech with no subject on "ngại lắm"; a reader supplies "she feels bad", natural enough.
