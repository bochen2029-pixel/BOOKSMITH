# Part One · Five Hours Apart

## I. Forty-One

Say it's a Tuesday in the last week of October, the odd week when London sits five hours ahead of Dallas instead of six, though you have no reason yet to know that.

You're on the forty-first floor. Everyone else left an hour ago, and the motion sensors have been putting the office to sleep behind you, one bank of lights at a time, until the only light left on the floor is yours. Somewhere down the hall a vacuum runs, stops, runs again. The dark monitors on the empty desks around you each hold a faint copy of you if you look at them, so you don't.

What the company does, if anyone asks at a party, is move money. What it actually does is move the information that moves money, a few hundred million times a day, and your job is to make sure the models that watch all that information are telling the truth. You build the tests that catch them when they aren't. It's a strange kind of work to explain. Most days you don't try.

What you're looking at is a test. For a week it has passed forty-nine times out of fifty, and the fiftieth time it fails for no reason anyone can find, which means there is a reason and nobody has found it yet. You've carried it to lunch and to the gym and into the shower, where most of your real thinking gets done. You carried it this afternoon through two meetings in which you nodded at the right moments and heard almost nothing.

At 6:52 you find it. Two processes, each convinced it got there first. Almost always, one of them is right. One run in fifty they cross in the dark, and everything downstream falls over in a way that looks like the model's fault and isn't.

The fix is four lines. You write them and run the test fifty times, then two hundred, and watch the results come up green, row after row of identical successes filling the screen like windows coming on in a tower at dusk.

Nobody will ever know which four lines. Sometime tomorrow someone will notice the test has stopped failing and assume it fixed itself, as people assume the lights come on by themselves. That's fine. This is the part you would do for free. It's the part you would do if no one paid you and no one ever found out: the moment something that was wrong becomes, under your hands, simply true.

You lean back, and the chair creaks, and you look out the window for the first time in hours.

The city has come on while you weren't watching. The freeways run white toward you and red away, braided where they cross, never quite still. West of downtown the Trinity is a long band of nothing, the one dark thing in the frame. Reunion Tower's globe cycles through its slow patterns of light. And farther out, past all of it, the landing lights hang in the dark in their patient lines.

Two lines, in fact. You can tell them apart without trying. The low ones, close and frequent, are Love Field's: 737s coming down over Bachman Lake every couple of minutes, the way they have your whole life. The high ones, far out past Irving, slow and evenly spaced, are DFW's. South wind tonight, so they're landing on the seventeens and eighteens, coming in over Grapevine. You know this the way you know your own address. You grew up in Euless, in the Mid-Cities, under the airport, and the sound of a heavy on final at three thousand feet is the closest thing to a lullaby you've ever had.

You've watched those lights from this window for years. You have never once wondered who was on them.

On the windowsill a black receiver the size of a thumb blinks once a second, as it has since June, listening to a sky it can't see. You don't look at it.

You pack the laptop. You find your badge. The elevator takes you down forty-one floors without a sound, and halfway there your ears do the small thing they do at the end of a flight.

P4 is four levels under the street, the bottom of the garage, where there's always a space and the air never moves. It smells like concrete and cooling engines. Your car sees you coming and unlocks, and its mirrors unfold like something waking up. Before you've touched anything, the screen has guessed: HOME, in white letters on the map, because it's after six on a weekday and it has learned what you do after six on a weekday. Twenty-five minutes, it says, straight up Stemmons, and it's usually right.

## II. Stemmons

Say the message comes on Stemmons.

But first you have to get out of the garage, and that part you do yourself. The car could probably manage it by now. It can back out of a space and find its own way to the street, and people post videos of it threading garages tighter than this one. But there's a difference between what a thing can do and what you'll let it do, and the spiral ramp up from four levels under the street, the ticket arm, and the blind right turn onto Commerce are three things you still like to do with your own hands. You take it up the spiral, past the same concrete pillars with the same scuffs at bumper height, and the arm lifts, and you are up and out in the last of the light.

Downtown at seven belongs to valets and cleaning crews and people walking dogs past dark lobbies. On a patio someone laughs at the end of a story you'll never hear the beginning of. Above the old Magnolia Building the red Pegasus stands with its wings up, as it has, in one form or another, since 1934.

At the first red light you give the car the wheel. When the light changes, the wheel moves under your hands, and you let it, and then you take your hands away. Your job now is to watch, which turns out to be a different thing from driving. You see more.

On the screen beside you, the car redraws the world as it understands it: lanes as clean lines, other cars as smooth gray shapes with no faces and no drivers, a blue ribbon laid out ahead to show where it means to go. Every fraction of a second it imagines the next few seconds a dozen different ways and chooses one. You know roughly how it does this. You've read the papers and argued about the architectures over lunch. Knowing hasn't made it any less strange. It's the closest thing you know to watching a thought.

Stemmons carries you north out of the core. The Design District's warehouses slide by on your left, and beyond them, past the levee, the long dark of the floodway. Ahead the brake lights come on in waves, a mile of red flinching at once, and then let go. In the mirror Reunion's globe gets smaller and keeps glowing.

This is the part of the day that belongs to no one. You're not at work and not yet home, and nothing is asked of you except to sit here and be carried. You have always liked it more than you could explain to anyone.

You had plans tonight, if the kind you make with yourself count. On the desk at home there's a yoke clamped to the edge and a throttle quadrant beside it, and tonight was going to be Heathrow. You've been flying simulators since you were twelve, when the airport was the biggest thing in your world and the game was the only way to get inside it. These days you fly the big jets, and you fly them the way the real pilots mostly don't anymore, by hand, and what you fly is the arrival. Tonight was going to be the long descent over the Channel in the dark and the lights of London coming up under the nose, the river, the towers off the left, twenty-seven left with a crosswind if the weather wanted one. The approach is the only part that's ever interested you. Seven hours of ocean fly themselves.

Then the screen chimes. 7:06. Dana, who runs your group.

> *You still downtown? Iris Wren's flight sat on the ground at Heathrow for two hours. Lands 8:10 now, Terminal D. Her car service gave up and I've got a kid with a fever. Any chance you could grab her? She's at the hotel on Elm, right by the office.*
>
> *And feed her. On us.*

Iris Wren, from the London office, who presents to the review council at nine tomorrow morning, and whom you have never met. To you she's what most people at the company are: a name at the top of documents. Hers are the good kind, careful and a little dry, written by someone who checks her own math. Her profile photo is the size of a postage stamp. You couldn't pick her out of a crowd.

For about three seconds you resist. Heathrow was right there. Then the three seconds pass.

"Hey Grok," you say. The assistant answers in the unhurried voice you chose for it because it was the least eager of the options. "Take me to DFW. Terminal D, arrivals."

The blue ribbon on the screen lifts off the lanes for home, considers, and lays itself down again bending west toward the Carpenter Freeway. The car, which has no opinions about anything, takes the new ribbon without comment. You dictate two words back to Dana, *On it*, and watch the lanes for home continue north without you.

Through Irving the towers of Las Colinas stand lit and mostly empty, their logos glowing for no one. Then the sky ahead changes. Over the airport the dark goes the dull amber of a place that never fully turns off, and there they are, the landing lights you sorted from the forty-first floor, close now. Four, five of them, strung out toward the runways, so evenly spaced they look like one slow thought being thought very carefully. Each of them holds a hundred people, or two hundred, or three. Each of those people is coming from somewhere, and being met, or not.

## III. International Parkway

Say it's eight minutes to eight when you come through the south toll plaza and down International Parkway.

The airport is bigger than Manhattan. You've known that your whole life as trivia, and you feel it now, with the road running straight up the middle of the place and the terminals curving away on either side, each one a long lit crescent, like ships moored along a dark canal. They built them as half circles so the walk from the car to the gate would be short, by people who assumed everyone would always drive. You know this place the way you know a house you grew up in. You know that the train runs inside security and the bus runs outside it, that the garages sit inside the crescents, that D is on the left going north, that the cell lot fills up by eight on a Friday. Out beyond the terminals, aircraft move across the field slowly and deliberately, as though the ground were deep water.

You leave the car on the fourth level of the Terminal D garage, between a minivan and a black sedan whose driver is asleep with his seat tipped all the way back, and you walk in.

Inside, it's the hour when an airport stops performing. Shops are pulling down their grilles. A man rides a floor polisher in slow loops across the terrazzo. The announcements come less often and sound like they're being made to an empty room. Up on the board, beside LONDON HEATHROW, the word DELAYED flickers and becomes LANDED 8:14, and then, a while later, AT GATE.

She'll have come in on a triple-seven, the big one, the one with the raked wingtips, and you know the choreography from here. The walk off the jet bridge. The lines at passport control, which at this hour are short but never nothing. The wait at the carousel. Then the frosted doors. Forty minutes if everything goes right, and you have never once seen everything go right.

So you have forty minutes, and a long day behind you, and you find the vending machines down by baggage claim, lit up blue in an alcove like a small aquarium. You press the buttons for the orange can, the Ultra Sunrise, and it drops with that heavy hollow sound, and it's cold enough to hurt your hand a little. You crack it standing right there. The hiss. The first long swallow, citrus and cold and faintly, pleasantly chemical, like something built in a lab by people who liked you. Three minutes later it arrives behind your eyes, a lift, a brightening, as if someone turned the gain up on the whole terminal. You weren't tired, and now you're less tired than that. It's a small pleasure and a cheap one and completely reliable.

International arrivals come out downstairs, through a pair of frosted doors that slide open every minute or two, release a few people blinking into the light, and close again. In front of the doors, behind a rail, a crowd has gathered. There are drivers in dark suits holding tablets with names on them. There's a family with a handmade sign in purple glitter, an old man in a pressed shirt with grocery-store flowers still in their plastic sleeve, and two small boys taking turns climbing the rail until their mother stops them and they start again.

Each time the doors open, the whole crowd leans in. Each time, someone's face changes.

A young woman comes through, and the old man with the flowers makes a sound you weren't meant to hear, and you look away, and then you look back. A teenager walks straight past his mother, gets ten steps, then turns and comes back into her arms as if he'd only been pretending not to know her. The boys' father comes through in a wrinkled suit and goes down on one knee, and they hit him at full speed, and he lets them knock him over.

Everyone here is waiting for someone they love.

You're waiting for a stranger.

When you were small your father used to drive you out to the north side of the airport on Saturday mornings, to the observation plaza where the speakers play the tower, and the two of you would sit on the hood of the car and watch them come in two hundred yards away while a calm voice overhead told each one it was cleared to land. He knew the aircraft by their sounds. He'd say *heavy* before you could see it, and he was always right. Years later, when you came home from the year in Singapore with everything you owned in two suitcases, he was at this rail, in a jacket he'd worn for the occasion, and he'd been there an hour because he didn't trust the traffic. He doesn't drive at night anymore. He says it's his eyes. You've never asked him how long he stood here, and he's never said, and you understand now, standing where he stood, that he wouldn't have minded.

You take out your phone. You open a blank note, type IRIS WREN, and make the letters as large as they'll go. Then you hold it up at chest height and feel every bit as ridiculous as you expected to. One of the drivers in the dark suits glances at you, at your phone, and gives you a brief professional nod, one colleague to another.

The doors open. Close. Open. A balloon slips out of somebody's fist and rises to the ceiling, forty feet up, where it stays. Your arm gets tired, and you switch arms, and you find you don't mind any of it. Every time the doors open and someone is found, a little of it reaches you secondhand, a borrowed share of every reunion in the room. By now you've had dozens.

## IV. The Doors

You don't recognize her. There's nothing to recognize; a postage stamp isn't a face. What you recognize is the way she comes through, which is the way she writes. She isn't looking for anyone. ## VI. Forty-Eight

The elevator is mirrored and very fast. Your ears do the thing again, in reverse. Less than three hours ago you came down a tower four blocks from here, alone.

The restaurant is nearly empty at this hour, which makes it feel as though it's been kept open for you: low light, dark wood, white linen, and glass from floor to ceiling. The host walks you to a table against the window, and the city lays itself out below you to its edges. There are the freeways you drove tonight, braided and moving, and the black band of the Trinity. Far off to the west is the faint amber of the airport, where an hour ago you stood with your arm in the air.

Below, a couple of blocks off, the red Pegasus stands on its roof with its wings up. You've looked up at that horse your whole life. This is the first time you've looked down at it.

"Is that a horse?" she asks. "With wings?"

"Pegasus. An oil company put it up there in 1934. It was the tallest thing in town. Pilots used it to find the city at night."

"It's still there."

"That one's a copy. The original rusted out, and they took it down and put that up, and then they found the original in a shed and fixed it, and now it's on the ground in front of a hotel by the convention center, where you can walk right up to it."

"Why a flying horse, though?"

You open your mouth and find you have nothing. "I know everything about it except that."

She seems to like this better than an answer would have been.

In the window her reflection sits over the city, a little transparent, the lights of downtown showing through it, and for a moment that's what you're looking at instead of the skyline. Then the waiter arrives, and you look at the menu.

You talk like two people who have a company in common and nothing else yet: Dana, the London office, the weather, the flight, the specific misery of the middle seat, which she didn't have this time but has had before and has opinions about. She looks at you for a moment longer than the sentence needed.

Neither of you says anything for a while after that, and it isn't awkward, which surprises you. Below the window a train slides along Pacific Avenue, lit and empty. Out west the amber over the airport hasn't changed. It won't.

"It's four in the morning in me," she says eventually. "Do you know the only thing I like about jet lag? For a day or so you're not anywhere. You're between."

"It's that week for everybody. Your clocks changed and ours haven't."

"Five hours instead of six."

"Until Sunday."

"Until Sunday," she agrees, and looks back out at the city, and that is all either of you says about it.

The check comes, and you hand over the company card, as instructed. By the time the waiter brings it back it's after eleven here, which makes it after four in her body, and it's beginning to show. She's still bright, but bright like a phone at two percent.

"I won't sleep," she says. "Not for an hour at least. I know how this goes." She looks down at the window. "Could I see the horse? From underneath?"

## VII. Elm Street

Downtown at a quarter past eleven on a Tuesday is a stage after the play. The streets are warm and nearly empty, and the stoplights go through their changes for nobody. A man hoses down a patio, and the smell of wet concrete comes across the street to meet you. A security guard in a lobby lifts two fingers from his desk as you pass, and you lift two back.

She walks with her coat over her arm, still, as if she's been carrying it so long she's forgotten it's there. She walks the way she came through the doors: reading everything.

"Is it always this empty?"

"At night. In the daytime it's hotter."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one anybody from here ever gives."

A train comes along Pacific as you cross it, two cars, lit the way a kitchen is lit at three in the morning, with no one on it at all, and slides away toward the West End.

"Would you ever leave?" she asks. "Dallas."

"I think about it the way I think about learning the piano."

"Which is?"

"Fondly. Every few years."

She laughs, and the laugh goes up the empty street and doesn't come back.

Then you're under it. From the forty-eighth floor it was a toy, something you could have picked up. From the street it's forty feet of red neon on a steel derrick, turning so slowly you have to watch a while to be sure, two horses back to back so that whichever way you're facing there's one coming toward you. The red gets on everything: the sidewalk, the windows across the street, her face when she tips it back to look.

"It's bigger from down here."

"Most things are."

"It's going round."

"It's always gone round."

She watches it make most of a turn. "I'd have used it too," she says. "If I were flying in. I'd have aimed for that."

Back at the hotel the doors are the revolving kind, and the lobby beyond them is all marble and hush. She turns and offers her hand, and you shake it, a handshake of exactly the right length.

"Seven," she says.

"Seven."

"Thank you," she says. "For the sign."

The door turns, and takes her, and turns again empty. All night you watched doors open and close on people you didn't know. This time you know who's behind them.

## VIII. Four in the Morning

Upstairs, in a room on the twenty-second floor with the curtains open, Iris Wren does not sleep.

She has taken off her shoes and nothing else. The suitcase stands against the wall where the bellman left it, listing on its wheel. The coat is on the chair. The bed is still made, and she is sitting on the end of it with her hands in her lap, looking at the city.

From here the horse is nearly at eye level, a few blocks off, turning. A train goes along Pacific, two cars, lit and empty, and the buildings take it. The freeway runs white one way and red the other. Far out, where he pointed, the sky over the airport is a dull amber that doesn't change.

Her phone is face down on the nightstand. She doesn't turn it over.

The horse goes round. She watches it make a turn, and another, and if she is counting them the number doesn't matter. Once she looks at the coat on the chair, and once at the suitcase, and once at her own hands, and then back at the city. She doesn't get up. She doesn't try to sleep. She knows how this goes.

At 4:31 by her body and 11:31 by the clock on the nightstand she is still there, with the horse going round and the city laid out to its edges, and she is not waiting for anything, and nothing is due.

## IX. Stemmons, Again

The valet brings the car around. The night hasn't cooled at all. You get in, and the screen offers you what it always offers: HOME, the blue ribbon, the smooth gray world, the car's careful dozen futures a second. You leave it there. You put both hands on the wheel, and you drive.

Stemmons, north. The same warehouses, the same levee, the same dark floodway on your left, and Reunion's globe in the mirror, getting smaller, still glowing.
