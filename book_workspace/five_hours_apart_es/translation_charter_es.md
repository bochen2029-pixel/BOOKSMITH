# Charter: *Five Hours Apart* in Latin American Spanish (es-419) — *Cinco horas de diferencia*

The law of this edition (BOOK_TRANSLATION_METHOD_v3 §6.1, §8). The SOURCE outranks this charter; this charter
outranks the registry's convenience; every change to it is a §15 line. Source: `_key/segments.jsonl` (515 segments,
22 units; identical to the file both Chinese editions were made from).

## §1. The book, in one paragraph

A Dallas evaluation engineer drives to DFW one Tuesday in the odd week of October (London five hours ahead, not six)
to pick up Iris Wren, a colleague from the London office he has never met; they eat, they find her bug (a six-hour
offset in a pipeline), they walk to the red Pegasus, they part. Part Two is the Friday she flies home: he shows her the
field in the smallest room under the building, a machine of colored knots that listens to the sky and keeps rows of
what it concludes; it is wrong for eleven minutes when the wind turns. Part Three is the field ten trillion years on,
running that evening again in rows, and rendering her. The book is told to "you", in the present tense, dry and
warm, with almost nothing explained.

## §2. Reader, locale, bar (R1)

- **es-419**: Latin American Spanish, leaning Mexican where a choice must be made, neutral where Mexican usage is
  strongly regional. The reader is a Spanish-speaking adult in the Americas, including US Hispanic readers in Texas.
- Title **Cinco horas de diferencia**; subtitle *Un supuesto, en tres partes*; Part One shares the book title.
- The bar: it must read as if written in Spanish by a writer with the author's restraint. No gloss, no footnote,
  no explanation the English does not give. One English sentence becomes one Spanish sentence wherever Spanish allows.

## §3. Narrative voice (R2)

- The narration addresses **tú**, in the **present tense** (presente narrativo), as the English does; past events
  inside the present take the pretérito / imperfecto as the English does.
- **Iris and the narrator use tú with each other from the first line to the last.** No usted→tú arc: the English has
  no T/V distinction and the switch would announce a closeness the author leaves undecided. The father and son use
  tú. Plural "you" is **ustedes** (never vosotros).
- Spanish is pro-drop: subject pronouns (tú, yo, él, ella) only where English stress or clarity needs them. Never
  "tú" at the head of every sentence of narration.
- Possessives drop on body parts and clothes ("levantas la mano", not "tu mano").
- Dry, never cold: understatement stays understated; no intensifiers the English lacks (muy, realmente, totalmente,
  completamente are spent only where the English spends them).

## §4. The device: "Say …" = «Digamos que …» (R3)

Every chapter that opens "Say it's …" opens **"Digamos que es …"**; Part Three is **"Tercera parte · Digamos"**; its
chapters open "Digamos que es más tarde." The opener of I is locked: *Digamos que es un martes de la última semana de
octubre, la semana desfasada, cuando Londres está cinco horas por delante de Dallas en lugar de seis, aunque todavía
no tienes ningún motivo para saberlo.* Fork C quotes it as «Digamos que es un martes…».

## §5. Dialogue (R4; the em-dash rule re-scoped for Spanish)

- **Raya (—) for dialogue, per the RAE.** Each paragraph that opens with speech opens with "—" (no space after it).
  The narrator's inciso goes between rayas: `—Soy yo —dice—. Nunca…` (period after the closing raya when the speech
  continues as a new sentence; a comma or nothing when it continues the same sentence).
- Speech **inside a narration paragraph** (a paragraph that does not open with speech) takes comillas “ ” — the
  paragraph cannot be split (block parity), and a raya mid-narration reads as the English em dash this kit bans.
- **Interrupted speech** takes the ellipsis, never a dash: "Is it—" → `—¿Es…?`; the completed line → `—…entonces no es
  medianoche —dice despacio—.` The three cut-off sites: V .003, VI .035–036, VI .072.
- The em dash appears **only** as the dialogue raya (and as the author's null in XVI's depth row). Anywhere else the
  gate fails it.
- Quoted words and titles in narration take “ ”; the dictated text and the slide title keep the English italics.
- ¿ ? and ¡ ! always paired; the one-word replies stay one word ("—Sí.", "—Más o menos.", "—Desde adentro.").

## §6. Punctuation, numerals, units (R10, R11)

- Ellipsis is the single character … ; no "..." anywhere.
- Clock times as the English writes them: digits where the English uses digits ("A las 6:52", "8:10", "11:31"),
  words where it uses words ("las siete y cuarto"). Spoken quarter-to times in the Latin American way: "veinte para
  las cinco", "ocho para las ocho".
- Years in digits (1867, 1934, 2019) unless the English spells them.
- **Fahrenheit kept**, with the scale named once where a Celsius reader would otherwise misread it: IV "setenta y un
  grados Fahrenheit", X "79, 71, 64, 57 °F"; then bare degrees ("Cincuenta y cinco grados."). Feet (pies), miles
  (millas), dollars (dólares) kept. Machine units in the rows stay as printed (s, ms, µs, ns, h, y, K, W, min).
- Days and months lower case (martes, octubre); headings in sentence case.

- Exonyms where Spanish has them: Londres, el Támesis, el canal de la Mancha, Irlanda, Leeds, Singapur, el Atlántico,
  Manhattan, Heathrow. Texas / **tejano**.
- Brand and product names as printed: Grok, Ultra Sunrise, KELVIN (the label), IRIS WREN (the phone sign).

## §9. The technical layer and the field's words (R6, R7)

- **heavy** (the aircraft) = **pesado** (noun; ICAO Spanish category *pesada*), everywhere: "el sonido de un pesado
  en aproximación final a tres mil pies", the father's "—Un pesado, del sur.", "el pesado de las siete", the rows.
  No gloss. "Decía «pesado» antes de que lo vieras." / "—Dice *pesado* igual que tú."
- **Machine expectation vs human waiting (R7).** Spanish *esperar* is both "wait" and "expect", so the field never
  *espera*: it **prevé** (prever, previsión). "It doesn't wait. It had an expectation that hadn't closed." →
  "—No espera. Tenía una previsión que no se había cerrado." Iris's answer keeps the human word: "—Eso es esperar."
  XVIII: "le dije que eso era esperar".
- The field's vocabulary, prose and rows alike: campo (field), celda (cell), lámina (sheet), nudo (knot), borde
  (edge; "justo dentro del borde"), frente (frontier: "Frente 14"), carril (lane), fila (row), registro (the record),
  cinta (the tape), asentarse (settle), quieto (quiet), tallar (carve), bifurcar / bifurcación (fork), rebobinar
  (rewind), descartar (discard), fusionar (join), impulsar (drive), previsión (expectation), abierta / cumplida
  (open / met), custodio (keeper), lámpara (the lamp), cuarto (the room, and the simulated room).
- "Quiet" for the field is **quieto** (still); XII .008 "She's quiet." → "Se queda quieta." keeps the author's echo.
- Lay words where the author is lay; engineer's words where he is an engineer: sandbox, hash, kernel, tabla de
  páginas, fork → "lo bifurcas", bits, bytes, pódcast.

## §12. Meaning rulings imported from the zh-Hant charter (language-neutral; they decide what the English says)

- XVIII .005 "There isn't a row for it": *it* is the hour at the rail, not the field → "No hay una fila para eso."
- XVIII .006 "he says it would matter to me": reported speech, *me* = Iris → "él dice que a mí me importaría".
- XII .024 "It was there before you were": presence, not arrival → "—Estaba ahí antes que tú."; XII .025 "It's always
  there" escalates it.
- XIV .012 "and you don't, for a while": looks forward (you keep moving for a while yet).
- XII .017 "hold one open for three [hours]": the row word *open* (abierta) carries into the prose.
- XIII .021: "He has a copy." is the narrator; after "She looks at you." the narrator keeps speaking ("Of the whole
  thing…").
- VIII .004 "where he pointed": a source defect (he never visibly points in VI), translated as written ("donde él
  señaló").
- III .015 / IX .006: the tired arm is the same phrase at both sites ("el brazo se te cansa, cambias de brazo").
- VI .042 "helpless and a little appalled": appalled at the bug, not incredulous ("sin poder parar y un poco
  espantada").
- VII .012 "Fondly.": warm and idle, not longing ("Con cariño.").
- VI .089 "Could I see the horse?": her own request ("¿Puedo ver el caballo?").
- I .007 the windows of "a tower at dusk": a tower (torre / edificio alto), as I .003's forty-one floors.
- III .013 "He doesn't drive at night anymore. He says it's his eyes.": the father is alive; present tense.
- XII .008 "She's quiet." echoes the field's word (§9).
- The doors at arrivals are a pair (las puertas); the hotel door is one revolving door (la puerta giratoria).
- The train on Pacific is a train (el tren), not a tram; the airport train runs inside security (el tren).

## §13. AI-tells and calques (the gate's forbidden list; reviewers extend it)

Never: "en base a", "a nivel de" (figurative), "es por eso que", "hacer sentido", "eventualmente" (= eventually),
"realizar" (= realize), "asumir" (= suppose), "honestamente" (use "la verdad", "francamente"), "sin lugar a dudas",
"cabe destacar", "en el corazón de", "un tapiz de", gerundio de posterioridad, a chain of "de … de … de", passive
"fue + participio" where Spanish says "se" or the active, subject pronouns at the head of every sentence, possessives
on the body, the ASCII quote ", "..." for …, a double space in prose, vosotros forms, Spain-only vocabulary (§8).

## §14. Process (R15)

- P0 frozen (shared with the Chinese editions). P1 key: this charter + `_key/registry_es.tsv`, built by the moderator.
- P3 by the moderator alone: all 22 units translated in reading order by one mind (one voice for a 13,470-word book;
  the Chinese run's waves cost a usage limit). Each unit is gated to PASS before the next.
- P5 independent QA by agents that did not translate: a Latin American native-register read with seams (two halves),
  a blind back-translation (two halves) diffed by the moderator against the English, the rows check (mechanical + read).
- P6 the moderator's full read. P9 ship report, ledgers COMPLETE.
