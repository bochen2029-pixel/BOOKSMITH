### ch_06.001
## VI. Forty-Eight

### ch_06.002
The elevator has mirrors and is very fast. The ears do it to you again, in reverse. Less than three hours ago you came down from a tower four blocks from here, alone [masc.].

### ch_06.003
The restaurant is almost empty at this hour, which gives the feeling that they kept it open for you [sing.]: low light, dark wood, white tablecloths and glass from floor to ceiling. They take you [sing.] to a table by the window, and the city stretches out below to its edges. There are the highways that you drove tonight, braided and in motion, and the black strip of the Trinity. Very far away, to the west, is the faint amber of the airport, where an hour ago you were standing with your arm raised.

### ch_06.004
Below, a couple of blocks away, the red Pegasus stands upright on its roof with its wings raised. You have looked up at that horse all your life. It is the first time that you look at it from above.

### ch_06.005
—Is that a horse? —she asks—. With wings?

### ch_06.006
—Pegasus. An oil company put it there in 1934. It was the tallest thing in the city. The pilots used it to find the city at night.

### ch_06.007
—It's still there.

### ch_06.008
—That one is a replica. The original rusted, and they took it down and put up that one, and then they found the original in a shed and they fixed it, and now it is on the ground in front of a hotel next to the convention center, where you can walk right up to it.

### ch_06.009
—And why a flying horse?

### ch_06.010
You open your mouth and discover that you have nothing. “I know everything about it except that.”

### ch_06.011
She seems to like that more than she would have liked an answer.

### ch_06.012
In the window her reflection rests on the city, a little transparent, with the lights of downtown passing through it, and for a moment it is that which you look at instead of the city's silhouette. Then the waiter arrives, and you look at the menu.

### ch_06.013
They chat [ambiguous: "Platican" = they chat / you (pl.) chat] like two people who have a company in common and nothing else yet: Dana, the London office, the weather, the flight, the specific misery of the middle seat, which this time did not fall to her but which she has had before and about which she has opinions. By the time the bread arrives, the conversation has already found its rhythm. She grew up in Leeds and has been living in London for twelve years and still gets lost south of the river, which according to her is a London tradition. She asks you how long you have been in Dallas.

### ch_06.014
—Always. Leaving out [lit. removing] Singapore.

### ch_06.015
—That's unusual, isn't it? In your line [of work].

### ch_06.016
—Everyone I work with is from somewhere else. It's like being the only one at the party who lives in the house.

### ch_06.017
—And is that lonely?

### ch_06.018
You tell her about the sensors. How the floor goes dark behind you one block at a time, and how somewhere there is a setting for that, and how never, not once, have you asked anyone to change that setting. It is a good answer to a question that she didn't ask.

### ch_06.019
—That's how the lights work —she says.

### ch_06.020
—That's how the lights work.

### ch_06.021
—I asked you about you.

### ch_06.022
—I know. —You turn the glass—. It's something. I'm not sure [masc.] that it's that.

### ch_06.023
Her cell phone lights up on the tablecloth. She looks at it out of the corner of her eye, and something in her face closes a little.

### ch_06.024
—Sorry —she says—. Tomorrow.

### ch_06.025
—The presentation.

### ch_06.026
—The presentation —she says— has a hole, and I have been looking into the hole since Sunday.

### ch_06.027
She tells you about it. Her team runs a model that watches the transfers that circulate between the London systems and yours (pl.). Since Sunday night it has flagged the same hour every night: from midnight to one, London time. “It's the quietest hour we have,” she says, “and since Sunday it looks like rush hour.” Nothing went out to production over the weekend. Nothing changed. She retrained the model, checked the features, checked the data twice. “I checked it again on the plane, which is a very sad sentence to say out loud.” She turns her glass a quarter turn on the tablecloth. “Let's see. Your whole job is knowing when the models are wrong. What would you check?”

### ch_06.028
—When did it start?

### ch_06.029
—Sunday night.

### ch_06.030
—What else happened on Sunday?

### ch_06.031
She frowns. “Nothing. It was Sunday. I washed clothes [= did laundry].”

### ch_06.032
And there it is: the same click that you felt at your desk at 6:52, the quiet noise that a problem makes when it rolls over and shows you its belly. Only this time there is someone on the other side of the table to hear it.

### ch_06.033
—You (pl.) set the clocks back on Sunday —you say.

### ch_06.034
She opens her mouth, and she closes it.

### ch_06.035
—Ours don't go back until next Sunday. This week you (pl.) are five hours ahead of us, not six. If something in that pipeline converts our time to yours (pl.) by adding six to it…

### ch_06.036
—…then it isn't midnight —she says slowly—. It's eleven.

### ch_06.037
—It's eleven. And at eleven there is a lot of movement [= activity].

### ch_06.038
—So it isn't wrong about the traffic.

### ch_06.039
—It isn't wrong about the traffic. It is wrong about the time.

### ch_06.040
She puts both hands flat on the tablecloth, as if to hold up the table. “We went out to production [= went live] in May.”

### ch_06.041
—Then it has never seen a time change.

### ch_06.042
—Never —she says— has it seen a time change. —Then she laughs, this time for real, unable to stop and a little horrified, so loudly that a couple two tables away looks up from their dessert.

### ch_06.043
You see how the week leaves her. It goes first from her shoulders, then from her jaw, then from the faint crease between her eyebrows that you had assumed was simply her face. What you feel, while you watch it go [ambiguous: "la ves irse" = watch it (the week) go, or watch her go], is not exactly pride, and it is not exactly the click from your desk either. It is warmer than that, and it is secondhand. It reaches you through her, the way the face of the older gentleman in front of the arrivals doors reached you, and it bears no proportion to what you actually did, which was to ask her what day it was.

### ch_06.044
—The fix is one line —she says.

### ch_06.045
—The fix is one line. Finding every place where someone wrote a six is everything else.

### ch_06.046
—I present at nine.

### ch_06.047
You hear yourself say it before having decided it. “I'll be there [lit. I arrive] at seven. We're going to find the sixes.”

### ch_06.048
—You don't have to do that.

### ch_06.049
—I know.

### ch_06.050
You are going to have to get up at five-thirty. You already regret it a little, the way you always regret the needle a little when you donate blood: a small, stinging cost, paid on purpose, for a feeling that you have never managed to put into words and that you already stopped trying.

### ch_06.051
The plates come and go. At some point the conversation completely stops being about the company, and neither of the two notices when it happens.

### ch_06.052
—You said something in the car —she says—. That you trust it more or less as much as anything that you understand almost completely. What is the part that you don't understand?

### ch_06.053
—Whether something is happening in there.

### ch_06.054
—Happening.

### ch_06.055
—Everyone I work with wants to know what it can do. Whether it gets it right, whether it is safe, whether it is cheaper than the previous one. Those are the questions they pay me for. The one that no one pays me [for] is whether something is like something for it [lit. for him; = whether there is something it is like, for it]. When it gets something wrong, is that something? When the elevator goes down, do its ears pop [lit. do its ears unblock]?

### ch_06.056
—Do you think so?

### ch_06.057
—The ones we make, no. Not yet. But I don't think it is the material. I think it is the form. I think that if you managed to make the processing be the right one, the kind of thing that makes itself, it would be there, in silicon or in flesh or in a sheet of whatever, and it wouldn't care what it is running on. —You hear your own voice and you lower it—. And you would never know it. That's the part. You could build it and sit across the table from it and never know it.

### ch_06.058
—Would it matter? If you couldn't tell it apart.

### ch_06.059
—To that, it would matter [lit. to that it would matter to it].

### ch_06.060
She says nothing for a moment, and you have time to hear what you have just done, which is to give a speech at a dinner to a woman who has been awake for twenty-four hours.

### ch_06.061
—Sorry. That's the speech that I don't give to anyone.

### ch_06.062
—I noticed that you had it ready.

### ch_06.063
—Since 2019, more or less.

### ch_06.064
She laughs, and it is not the polite laugh. And there is in this a pleasure that is not the fix and is not the view and is not that thing that you noticed at the doors and from which you looked away. It is the pleasure of giving someone what matters to you and seeing that they hold it properly. You had forgotten [lit. it had forgotten itself to you] that it was on the list.

### ch_06.065
—What would you have been doing tonight? —she asks—. If Dana hadn't sent you a message.

### ch_06.066
—The truth?

### ch_06.067
—The truth.

### ch_06.068
—I would have flown to London.

### ch_06.069
She waits. [ambiguous: "Espera." = she waits, or imperative "Wait."]

### ch_06.070
—In a simulator. On the computer at home [lit. the computer of the house]. It's what I do instead of watching television. Tonight was going to be the approach to Heathrow.

### ch_06.071
—You were going to land at Heathrow tonight.

### ch_06.072
—On two-seven left. There is almost always wind from the west. You come in over the city…

### ch_06.073
—I know how one comes in. I have done it a hundred times.

### ch_06.074
—From inside.

### ch_06.075
—From inside. You see a seatback and a man's elbow.

### ch_06.076
—From the front they are the best ten minutes that exist. Seven hours of nothing, and then the whole city comes out of the darkness and you have ten minutes not to ruin it [ambiguous: "echarla a perder" — "la" = the city, or the approach implied].

### ch_06.077
—But why the landing? Of everything.

### ch_06.078
You think about it, because she asked it as if she wanted the answer for real. “The cruise flies itself. No one is needed. The landing is the only part in which it matters that there is someone in the seat.”

### ch_06.079
She looks at you a moment longer than the sentence needed.

### ch_06.080
—So you have seen my city from the front a hundred times —she says—, and you have never been in it.

### ch_06.081
—And you have been in it twelve years and you have never seen it from the front.

### ch_06.082
Neither of the two says anything for a while after that, and it is not uncomfortable, which surprises you. Below the window a train glides along Pacific Avenue, lit up and empty. To the west, the amber over the airport has not changed. It is not going to change.

### ch_06.083
—In my body it is four in the morning —she says at last—. Do you know what the only thing I like about jet lag [lit. time-zone lag] is? For a day or so you are nowhere. You are in between.

### ch_06.084
—It's that week for everyone. Their clocks changed and ours didn't. [ambiguous: "Sus relojes" = their clocks / your (pl.) clocks]

### ch_06.085
—Five hours instead of six.

### ch_06.086
—Until Sunday.

### ch_06.087
—Until Sunday —she concedes, and she looks at the city again, and that is all that either of the two says about it.

### ch_06.088
The check arrives, and you hand over the company card, per the instructions. By the time the waiter brings it back it is already past eleven here, which means that in her body it is past four, and it is starting to show. She still shines, but she shines like a cell phone at two percent.

### ch_06.089
—I'm not going to sleep —she says—. Not in an hour at least [= not for at least an hour]. I know how this goes. —She looks down through the window—. Can I see the horse? From below?

#### notes ch_06
- "La que nadie me paga es si algo es como algo para él.": "por" is missing ("Por la que nadie me paga"), and "si algo es como algo para él" is opaque in Spanish (a calque of "what it is like").
- "por una sensación que nunca has logrado poner en palabras y que ya dejaste de intentar": the second relative clause does not fit "intentar" (dangling object).
- "mientras la ves irse": "la" can be the week or Iris.
- Continuity: "una mujer que lleva veinticuatro horas despierta" here vs "veinte horas despierta" in ch_05.002, roughly two hours earlier in the story.
- "Sus relojes cambiaron y los nuestros no.": "Sus" splits between "their" and "your (pl.)".
