### ch_01.001
## I. Forty-One

### ch_01.002
Let's say it is a Tuesday in the last week of October, the out-of-phase week [= the week the clocks are out of sync], when London is five hours ahead of Dallas instead of six, although you still have no reason to know it.

### ch_01.003
You are on floor forty-one. Everyone else left an hour ago, and the motion sensors have been putting the office behind you to sleep, one block of lights at a time, until the only light left on the floor is yours. Somewhere along the hallway a vacuum cleaner starts, stops, starts again. The switched-off monitors of the empty desks around you each keep a faint copy of you if you look at them, so you don't look at them.

### ch_01.004
What the company does, if someone asks at a party, is move money. What it does in reality is move the information that moves the money, a few hundred million times a day, and your job is to make sure that the models that watch over all that information tell the truth. You build the tests that catch them when they don't tell it. It is a strange job to explain. Most days you don't even try.

### ch_01.005
What you are looking at is a test. For a week now it passes [= has been passing] forty-nine out of every fifty times, and the fiftieth time it fails for no reason that anyone finds, which means that there is a reason and no one has found it yet. You have taken it with you to lunch and to the gym and to the shower, where almost everything you truly think happens. This afternoon you carried it through two meetings in which you nodded at the right moments and heard almost nothing.

### ch_01.006
At 6:52 you find it. Two processes, each one convinced that it arrived first. Almost always one of the two is right. In one out of every fifty runs they cross in the dark, and everything that follows collapses in a way that looks like the model's fault and isn't.

### ch_01.007
The fix is four lines. You write them and you run the test fifty times, then two hundred, and you see how the results turn green, row after row of identical successes filling the screen like the windows that light up in a tower at nightfall.

### ch_01.008
No one will ever know which four lines. Tomorrow, at some point, someone will notice that the test stopped failing and will assume that it fixed itself, the way people assume that the lights turn themselves on. That's fine. This is the part you would do for free. It is the part you would do even if no one paid you and no one ever found out: the moment in which something that was wrong becomes, under your hands, simply true.

### ch_01.009
You lean back, and the chair creaks, and you look out the window for the first time in hours.

### ch_01.010
The city lit up while you weren't looking. The highways run white in your direction and red in the other, braided where they cross, never entirely still. West of downtown, the Trinity is a long strip of nothing, the only dark thing in the picture. The sphere of the Reunion tower slowly runs through its patterns of light. And farther away, beyond everything, the landing lights hang in the darkness in their patient rows.

### ch_01.011
In fact, they are two rows. You tell them apart without effort. The low ones, close and one right after another, are Love Field's: 737s that come down over Lake Bachman every couple of minutes, as they have done all your life. The high ones, very far away, beyond Irving, slow and evenly spaced, are DFW's. Tonight there is wind from the south, so they land on the seventeens and the eighteens, coming in over Grapevine. You know it the way you know your own address. You grew up in Euless, in the Mid-Cities, under the airport, and the sound of a heavy [ambiguous: "un pesado" = a heavy aircraft, or colloquially a bore / a pest] on final approach at three thousand feet is the closest thing to a lullaby you have ever had.

### ch_01.012
You have spent years looking at those lights from this window. Not a single time have you wondered who was in them [lit. who was going in them].

### ch_01.013
On the windowsill, a black receiver the size of a thumb blinks once per second, as it does [= has done] since June, listening to a sky that it cannot see. You don't look at it.

### ch_01.014
You put away the laptop. You find the badge. The elevator takes you down forty-one floors without a sound, and halfway down your ears do that small thing they do at the end of a flight.

### ch_01.015
P4 is four levels below the street, at the bottom of the parking garage, where there is always a spot and the air never moves. It smells of concrete and of engines that are cooling. Your car sees you coming and unlocks, and the mirrors unfold like something that wakes up. Before you touch anything, the screen already guessed: HOME, in white letters over the map, because it is a weekday and it is past six, and it already learned what you do on a weekday after six. Twenty-five minutes, it says, straight down the Stemmons, and it is almost always right.

#### notes ch_01
- "el sonido de un pesado en aproximación final": read cold, "un pesado" first suggests the colloquial "a bore / a pest"; the aviation sense ("a heavy") only lands from context.
- "Nadie sabrá nunca cuáles cuatro líneas.": "cuáles" + noun is colloquial (fine in Mexico); "qué cuatro líneas" is the neutral norm.
- "la semana desfasada": understandable but slightly opaque; it reads as "the out-of-sync week" only once the next clause explains it.
