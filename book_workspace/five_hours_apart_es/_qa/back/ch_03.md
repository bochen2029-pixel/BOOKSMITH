### ch_03.001
## III. International Parkway

### ch_03.002
Let's say that it is eight to eight [= 7:52] when you go through the south toll booth and take International Parkway.

### ch_03.003
The airport is bigger than Manhattan. You have known it all your life as a curious fact, and now you feel it, with the road running straight through the middle of the place and the terminals curving on each side, each one a long lit half-moon, like ships moored along a dark canal. They built them in a half circle so that the path from the car to the boarding gate would be short, people who took for granted that everyone was always going to arrive driving. You know this place the way one knows a house one grew up in. You know that the train runs inside the security zone and the bus outside, that the parking garages are inside the half-moons, that D is on the left going north, that the waiting lot [= cell-phone lot] fills up before eight on Fridays. Beyond the terminals, the airplanes move along the runways slowly and carefully, as if the ground were deep water.

### ch_03.004
You leave the car on the fourth level of the Terminal D parking garage, between a minivan and a black sedan whose driver sleeps with the seat reclined all the way back, and you go in.

### ch_03.005
Inside it is the hour when an airport stops acting [= stops putting on a show]. The shops lower their metal shutters. A man drives a floor polisher in slow turns over the terrazzo. The announcements come further and further apart and sound as if they were being addressed to an empty room. Up on the board, next to LONDON HEATHROW, the word DELAYED blinks and turns into LANDED 8:14, and then, a while later, AT GATE.

### ch_03.006
She will have arrived [= must have arrived] on a triple seven, the big one, the one with the wingtips swept back, and from here you know the choreography by heart. The walk along the boarding bridge. The immigration lines, which at this hour are short but are never empty. The wait at the baggage belt. Then the frosted doors. Forty minutes if everything goes well, and you have never seen, not a single time, everything go well.

### ch_03.007
So you have forty minutes, and a long day on your back, and you find the vending machines down there, next to baggage claim, lit up blue in a niche like a small aquarium. You press the buttons for the orange can, the Ultra Sunrise, and it drops with that heavy, hollow noise, and it is so cold that your hand hurts a little. You open it right there, standing. The hiss. The first long swallow, citrus and cold and a little bit, pleasantly, chemical, like something manufactured in a laboratory by people who were fond of you. Three minutes later it reaches you behind the eyes, a rise, a glow, as if someone had turned up the gain on the whole terminal. You weren't tired [masc.], and now you are less tired than that. It is a small pleasure, and cheap, and completely reliable.

### ch_03.008
International arrivals come out downstairs, through a pair of frosted doors that open every one or two minutes, release a few people who blink at the light, and close again. In front of the doors, behind a railing, a crowd has gathered. There are drivers in dark suits with tablets that carry names. There is a family with a handmade sign in purple glitter, an older gentleman in an ironed shirt with supermarket flowers still in their plastic sleeve, and two small children who take turns climbing onto the railing until their mom stops them, and they start again.

### ch_03.009
Every time the doors open, the whole crowd leans forward. Every time, someone's face changes.

### ch_03.010
A young woman comes out, and the man with the flowers makes a sound that was not meant for you to hear, and you look away, and then you look again. A teenager [masc.] walks right past his mom, takes ten steps, then turns around and goes back into her arms as if he had only been pretending that he didn't know her. The children's dad comes out in a wrinkled suit and puts one knee on the floor, and they crash into him at full speed, and he lets himself be knocked over.

### ch_03.011
Everyone here is waiting for someone they love [ambiguous: "quieren" = love, or want].

### ch_03.012
You are waiting for a stranger [fem.: "una desconocida"].

### ch_03.013
When you were little [masc.: "chico"], your dad used to take you on Saturday mornings to the north side of the airport, to the observation plaza where the speakers broadcast the control tower, and the two would sit on the hood of the car to watch them land at two hundred yards while a calm voice, from above, told each one that it had clearance to land. He recognized the airplanes by the sound. He would say *heavy* before you saw it, and he was always right. Years later, when you came back home from your year in Singapore with everything you had in two suitcases, he was at this railing, in a jacket that he had put on for the occasion, and he had been there an hour because he didn't trust the traffic. He no longer drives at night. He says it's his eyes. You have never asked him how long he stood here, and he has never said it, and now you understand, standing [masc.] where he stood, that he wouldn't have minded [lit. it would not have mattered to him].

### ch_03.014
You take out your cell phone. You open a blank note, you write IRIS WREN and you make the letters as big as they can go. Then you raise it to chest height and you feel exactly as ridiculous [masc.] as you expected. One of the drivers in dark suits looks at you, looks at your phone, and gives you a brief professional gesture with his head [= nod], colleague to colleague.

### ch_03.015
The doors open. They close. They open. A balloon escapes from someone's fist and rises to the ceiling, forty feet up, where it stays. Your arm gets tired, and you switch arms, and you discover that none of this bothers you. Every time the doors open and find someone [ambiguous: subject of "encuentran" = the doors, or impersonal "people/they"], a little of that reaches you secondhand, a borrowed share of every reunion in the hall. By now dozens have already come your way [lit. "te han tocado" = have touched you / fallen to your lot].

#### notes ch_03
- "Las construyeron en medio círculo para que..., personas que daban por hecho que...": the trailing apposition "personas que..." hangs loosely after the purpose clause; reads awkward.
- "Todos aquí están esperando a alguien que quieren.": norm is "a alguien a quien quieren"; "quieren" also splits love/want.
- "Cada vez que se abren las puertas y encuentran a alguien": grammatically the doors are the ones who "find" someone; reads odd.
- Gender: "cansado", "chico", "parado", "ridículo" mark the "you" as male throughout.
