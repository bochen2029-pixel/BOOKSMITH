### ch_04.001
## IV. The Doors

### ch_04.002
Let's say that the doors open on her [= open to reveal her] at four past nine [lit. at nine and four].

### ch_04.003
You don't recognize her. There is nothing to recognize; a postage stamp is not a face. What you recognize is the way she comes out, which is the way she writes. She is not looking for anyone. She is looking at everything, reading the hall from left to right as if it were a page she intends to read properly. She carries a wool coat folded over one arm, a London coat, out of place here, and she drags a suitcase that tilts to one side on a broken wheel.

### ch_04.004
You register that she is beautiful the way the ears registered the descent from floor forty-one: not as a thought, only as a change that your body notices before you have decided anything about it. Then you look at the suitcase.

### ch_04.005
Her eyes find the cell phone. She stops in front of you, reads her own name and looks up.

### ch_04.006
—It's me —she says—. No one had ever waited for me [lit. they had never waited for me] with a sign.

### ch_04.007
—It's a cell phone.

### ch_04.008
—It's a sign —she says, gently, like someone who corrects a definition in a draft, and you decide that she is right.

### ch_04.009
You tell her your name. She repeats it carefully, like someone who intends to remember it. Her accent is something from the north that twelve years of London have flattened without erasing it entirely, and she pronounces your name as if it had one more syllable than the ones you have been giving it.

### ch_04.010
—Dana said that you offered yourself as a volunteer [masc.: "voluntario"; = you volunteered].

### ch_04.011
—Dana offered me as a volunteer [= Dana volunteered me].

### ch_04.012
—Then thank you for having been offered as a volunteer [= for having been volunteered]. —A smile, which appears and goes away—. And I am terribly sorry. You must have been here an hour.

### ch_04.013
—More or less.

### ch_04.014
—How awful [lit. what horror].

### ch_04.015
—It wasn't —you say, and you mean it [lit. you say it seriously], and you can't explain why without sounding strange, so you don't explain it.

### ch_04.016
You reach your hand toward the suitcase. She says that there is no need, that it is broken, that it embarrasses her. You say that you carry it [= you'll take it]. There is another round of the old ritual in which two strangers refuse and accept the same favor, and then she lets you [ambiguous: "te deja" = lets you, or leaves you], and the two cross the pedestrian bridge toward the parking garage, with the broken wheel ticking behind you (pl.) at every joint in the floor.

### ch_04.017
The parking garage is open to the night, and the air that crosses through it is at seventy-one degrees Fahrenheit and smells of jet fuel and of cut grass. She stops walking.

### ch_04.018
—It's hot —she says, like someone reporting a fault—. We are at the end of October.

### ch_04.019
—We are at the end of October in Texas.

### ch_04.020
She looks at the coat over her arm as if it had personally disappointed her.

#### notes ch_04
- "y luego ella te deja": read alone, "te deja" first suggests "she leaves you"; only the context gives "she lets you".
- "Registras que es hermosa como los oídos registraron el descenso": "los oídos" with no possessive is a little loose ("tus oídos" would anchor it).
